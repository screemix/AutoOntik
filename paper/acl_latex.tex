\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds large language model (LLM)
outputs in evidence retrieved at inference time \citep{lewis2020rag}, but
retrieval over unstructured, chunked text struggles with multi-hop
questions whose evidence is scattered across many documents. Graph-based
RAG addresses this by retrieving over a knowledge graph (KG) instead of
flat passages, exploiting entities, typed relations, and their
organization into type hierarchies to support multi-hop reasoning and
query-time generalization over specific mentions \citep{edge2024graphrag}.
The quality of graph-based retrieval is therefore bounded by the quality
of the KG that backs it: a graph with redundant, untyped nodes and
unconstrained edges offers little more structure than the text it was
extracted from.

Constructing a KG that actually supports this kind of retrieval requires
more than extracting entities and relations. It requires (i) a canonical,
deduplicated vocabulary of entity and relation surface forms, (ii) an
explicit \emph{is-a} hierarchy over entity types, so that a query about a
general category (e.g.\ \emph{artwork}) can retrieve instances of any of
its specializations without enumerating every leaf type by hand, and (iii)
relation-level domain and range constraints, so that a retrieved triple
can be validated against the schema the KG itself induces. Traditional
ontology engineering supplies all three by hand, at a cost that does not
scale to open-domain corpora; hand-curated top-level ontologies such as
DOLCE \citep{borgo2023dolce} offer stable, philosophically motivated
top-level structure but cannot be extended automatically to an arbitrary
corpus's own vocabulary.

Recent work instead automates (i) directly from text with LLMs. KGGen
\citep{mo2025kggen} extracts open-vocabulary triples and merges
near-duplicate entity and relation surface forms via embedding-based
clustering and LLM-adjudicated verification, producing a substantially
cleaner graph than raw extraction alone. This flat canonicalization is
exactly where KGGen -- and schema-free extraction pipelines like it --
stop: entities and relations are deduplicated, but the resulting graph
carries no type hierarchy and no domain/range constraints, precisely the
structure (ii) and (iii) above that graph-based retrieval depends on.

Extending flat canonicalization into a typed, hierarchical ontology
surfaces problems flat deduplication does not have to solve. Type labels
are themselves open-vocabulary free text and need the same
clustering-plus-LLM-verification treatment as entities and relations, but
naively merging similar type labels systematically conflates general
categories with their own specializations (e.g.\ \emph{film} and
\emph{documentary film}), since a specialization participates in nearly
every relation its parent does and is therefore embedding-similar to it by
construction. Inducing a hierarchy over the resulting canonical types is a
distinct problem again: a single-pass or bottom-up placement can
permanently misattach a type under too shallow a parent whenever a more
specific sibling type happens to be discovered later, with no mechanism to
correct the placement afterward. And because relation canonicalization
merges surface forms without regard to grammatical role, a canonical
relation can silently pool a predicate together with its grammatical
inverse, which fragments -- rather than reinforces -- the very
domain/range evidence constraint induction relies on.

We present \textbf{OntoDisco}, a pipeline that extends KGGen's schema-free
extract-then-canonicalize approach with the machinery needed to close this
gap. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A schema-free typed extraction and canonicalization pipeline that,
  in addition to a deduplicated entity and relation vocabulary, produces a
  canonical, hierarchically organized entity-type vocabulary and a set of
  per-relation domain/range constraints (\S\ref{sec:methods}).
  \item A hyponymy-aware type canonicalization step that combines
  relation-argument distributional evidence with an explicit
  \emph{kind-of} test in the verification prompt, so that specializations
  are not merged into their own parent category (\S\ref{sec:type-dedup}).
  \item A priority-ordered, top-down hierarchy induction procedure that
  places types from most to least general, by a Weeds-precision
  containment signal \citep{weeds2004characterising}, and that -- unlike a
  single-pass placement -- can retroactively insert a newly placed type
  \emph{above} an already-placed one, correcting an earlier too-shallow
  attachment rather than only ever preventing it going forward
  (\S\ref{sec:hierarchy-induction}).
  \item An entity deduplication step that resolves entity identity as the
  pair (name, canonical type) rather than name alone, and restricts
  embedding-based candidate comparison to a symbolic gate -- identical
  type or shared immediate parent in the induced hierarchy -- before
  clustering (\S\ref{sec:entity-dedup}).
  \item A relation-direction resolution step that detects and corrects
  merged grammatical inverses before mining domain/range constraints, and
  a hierarchy-generalized, PCA-style confidence score
  \citep{galarraga2013amie} for the resulting constraints
  (\S\ref{sec:constraints}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Methods: OntoDisco}
\label{sec:methods}

% \subsection{Overview}
% \label{sec:methods-overview}

OntoDisco turns a corpus of open-vocabulary into a canonicalized, typed knowledge graph: a deduplicated relation vocabulary, a deduplicated and hierarchically organized entity-type vocabulary, a deduplicated entity vocabulary with class assignments, and a set of relation-level domain/range constraints.
At first, a schema-free LLM extractor  (\S\ref{sec:extraction}) produces a collection of raw triplets: 
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G} = (s, t_s, r, o, t_o, Q),
\end{equation}
where $s$, -- subject entity, $t_s$ -- subject type, $r$ -- relation predicate, $o$ -- object entity, $t_o$ -- object type, $Q$ -- an optional list of qualifiers extracted from texts. Then $\mathcal{G}$ is passed further for canonization and typing. Every downstream stage resolves fields into canonical identifiers, keeping each stage's data dependencies explicit and independently inspectable.

The pipeline runs ordered stages, described in
\S\ref{sec:relation-dedup}--\S\ref{sec:constraints}: \emph{(1) relation
canonicalization}, \emph{(2) entity-type canonicalization}, which also
induces a taxonomy hierarchy over the resulting type vocabulary,
\emph{(3) entity deduplication and class assignment}, and \emph{(4)
domain/range constraint induction}. The ordering is dictated by data
dependencies. Entity-type canonicalization is conditioned on the canonical relation vocabulary as a disambiguating signal. Entity deduplication runs after the type hierarchy because it uses the hierarchy as a symbolic gate for candidate synonyms. Constraint induction derives relation constraints directly from the extracted triplets and the outputs of the first two stages.

The following design choices recur across stages. (1) Deduplication of synonimous surface forms is performed by clustering with Contriever sentence embeddings \citep{izacard2022contriever} and HDBSCAN
\citep{campello2013hdbscan} clustering. Next, every merge/split decision for deduplication is adjudicated by a single LLM call per candidate group.(2) Result of each stage is checkpointed once it completes, allowing of independent inspection of stages' results and re-using it in follow-up experiments.

\subsection{Initial Typed Triplet Extraction}
\label{sec:extraction}
Subject and object types are extracted as free text, not
constrained to any predefined schema. This schema-free design allows the canonical type vocabulary used throughout the rest of the pipeline to emerge from the data itself. The full extraction prompt is given in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompts}, Listing~\ref{lst:extraction}.

\subsection{Relation Deduplication}
\label{sec:relation-dedup}

Relation canonicalization maps the raw, free-text \texttt{relation} strings observed across all triplets onto a canonical relation vocabulary
$\mathcal{R}^*$. Candidate clusters are generated by embedding every raw
relation surface form and clustering with HDBSCAN as described
before. Every multi-member candidate cluster is then passed to a single LLM call that decides whether to merge all members into one relation or split them into several (Listing~\ref{lst:cluster-relations}).

\subsection{Entity Types Deduplication}
\label{sec:type-dedup}

This stage both flattens the raw type vocabulary into a canonical set
$\mathcal{T}^*$ and organizes $\mathcal{T}^*$ into a taxonomy hierarchy.

\subsubsection{Type canonicalization}

Candidate clusters over raw type labels are generated the same way as for
relations (\S\ref{sec:relation-dedup}), but with two additional sources of evidence. First, before LLM verification, any two candidate clusters whose relation-argument profiles (TF-IDF-weighted over which canonical relations each type's mentions occur as an argument of) are sufficiently cosine-similar are further merged. Second, each candidate label shown to the LLM verifier is annotated with a the relations it most often co-occurs with, giving the verifier concrete distributional evidence beyond the bare label string.

The verification prompt additionally enforces a hyponymy (``kind of'') test: two labels are merged only if neither is a hyponym of the other, even when their relation-argument profiles overlap almost entirely. For example, \ \emph{documentary film} and \emph{film} participate in the same relations, but LLM instructed to keep them distinct, whereas \emph{movie} and \emph{film}, true synonyms, should
be merged. The full prompt, including this hyponymy test, is given in Listing~\ref{lst:cluster-entity-types}.

\subsubsection{Hierarchy induction}
\label{sec:hierarchy-induction}

Flat canonicalization removes surface redundancy but does not recover
subsumption structure; a typed knowledge graph needs an explicit is-a
hierarchy over $\mathcal{T}^*$, both to gate candidate pairs during entity
deduplication (\S\ref{sec:entity-dedup}) and to generalize sparse
domain/range observations during constraint induction
(\S\ref{sec:constraints}). We induce this hierarchy with a single
priority-ordered, top-down, pairwise placement procedure:
we evaluated for this step.

% WHY NOT FLAT CLUSTERING?

\paragraph{Priority queue.} Every canonical type in $\mathcal{T}^*$ is
represented by a relation-argument profile, PPMI-weighted by default. A
one-shot pass based on the Weeds precision containment measure
\citep{weeds2004characterising} over the full, untouched pool computes,
for every type $u$, an in-degree score counting how many other types $v$
look containable within it:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weeds-indeg}
  \mathrm{deg_{in}}(u) = \bigl| \{\, v \neq u : \mathrm{WeedsPrec}(v, u) \geq
  \tau \,\} \bigr|.
  \\
\end{equation}
$\mathrm{WeedsPrec}(v, u)$ is the fraction of $v$'s relation-argument weight that $u$
also exhibits:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{WeedsPrec}(v, u) = \sum_{f \in F(v)} w_u(f) \big/ \sum_{f \in
F(v)} w_v(f),
\end{equation}
and $F(v)$ is the set of relation-argument features with nonzero
weight under $v$, and $\tau$ is a fixed containment threshold. The in-degree is a structural breadth signal, not a semantic judgment: types are grouped into priority bands from highest in-degree to lowest to identify high-level ontology concept first. If a set of external seed labels is supplied, each is matched to
$\mathcal{T}^*$ by normalized label (or synthesized fresh if unmatched) and pinned at the top of the queue as a root candidates with no other node can be a parent of which; without seeds, the top band of the same ranking performs root discovery instead.

\paragraph{Per-node placement.} Each node is routed top-down through
whatever higher-priority bands have already placed: at every level, the
remaining candidates (initially the whole pool; during descent, an
already-placed node's existing children) are shown to the LLM in
similarity-ranked batches (Listing~\ref{lst:hierarchy-pairwise}), and a response of ``none of these'' escalates to the next, less-similar batch (up to a fixed cap). Every
batch response is one of four outcomes:
% \textbf{synonym} of a candidate, \textbf{child of} a candidate, \textbf{parent of} a candidate, \textbf{none} of the candidates.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{synonym} of a candidate: merge the two synonymous nodes that were not captured in flat clustering deduplication.
  \item \textbf{child of} a candidate: descend one level deeper into that
  candidate's own children at the next iteration, rather than attaching
  immediately, so that a more specific attachment is never preempted.
  \item \textbf{parent of} candidates: insert the incoming node
  \emph{above} the defined candidates, detaching its existing parent edge (if any)
  and reattaching it beneath the new node, which then settles at the
  current level. 
  \item \textbf{none} of the candidates fit at any escalation batch: the
  node settles at its current position -- as a new root if this occurred at
  the top level, or as an ordinary child of its current descent anchor
  otherwise.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Depth bound and guarantees.} A maximum-depth cap parameter bounds the worst-case number of placement decisions for any candidate node. A node that would need to exceed it is placed into a structurally separate unresolved bucket rather than being force-attached or discarded, allowing to extent the resulted hieararchy later on. The result of such procedure is always a single-parent forest at every
point in the procedure: acyclicity and the absence of transitive shortcut
edges follow directly from the construction. 

\subsection{Entity Deduplication}
\label{sec:entity-dedup}

Entity deduplication canonicalizes entity mentions and  assigns each canonical entity types. An entity's identity is the compound pair (name, canonical type) rather than name alone, so
that homonymous names typed differently (e.g.\ \emph{Paris} the city versus
\emph{Paris} the person) are never conflated by construction.

Before embedding-based comparison, candidate pairs are restricted by a symbolic gate: two mentions are only ever compared if they share
literally the same type, or are siblings under the same immediate parent
in the induced type hierarchy (\S\ref{sec:hierarchy-induction}). HDBSCAN call only ever compares candidates within one sibling
group: two mentions in different partitions could never have merged under the gate regardless of embedding proximity.

Within each partition, merging proceeds over several rounds rather than a
single pass: each round re-embeds the current pool (already-merged
canonical entities plus untouched singleton mentions), re-clusters with
HDBSCAN, and sends multi-member clusters to the LLM for verification (Listing~\ref{lst:cluster-entity-names}), then folds accepted merges back into the pool for the next round. Rounds stop
once one produces no further reduction in pool size, or a fixed round cap
is reached.

\subsection{Constraints Construction}
\label{sec:constraints}

\paragraph{Stage 0: re-resolution.} For every raw triplet, its subject and
object type strings and its relation string are looked up against the
canonical vocabularies produced by \S\ref{sec:relation-dedup} and
\S\ref{sec:type-dedup}.

\paragraph{Stage 1: relation-direction resolution.} Because relation
canonicalization merges surface forms without regard to grammatical role
(\S\ref{sec:relation-dedup}), a canonical relation can silently pool a
predicate together with its grammatical inverse (e.g.\ \emph{directed} and
\emph{was directed by}), which would otherwise fragment one well-typed
relation's domain/range support across two competing, individually weak
orientations. 

% Subject/object type overlap cannot by itself distinguish this
% case from a genuinely order-free relation with identical domain and range
% types, since both situations produce the same marginal type statistics;
% direction is instead resolved from the signal that actually carries it, the literal surface form. 

For every canonical relation with more than one
surface form, a single LLM call classifies each surface form against the
canonical label as \emph{forward} or \emph{inverted} (Listing~\ref{lst:relation-direction}); triplets whose
original predicate was classified inverted have their subject and object
types swapped before the next stage. This catches merged grammatical
inverses.

\paragraph{Stage 2: hierarchy generalization.} For each canonical
relation's domain (subject-role types) and range (object-role types),
considered independently, we compute the exact lowest common ancestor of
all observed types in the single-parent hierarchy. If the observed types span disconnected
parts of the hierarchy forest, they are partitioned by which root each
descends from and an LCA is computed independently within each partition,
yielding more than one domain (or range) signature and hence more than
one constraint for relations that genuinely connect unrelated type
families.

\paragraph{Stage 3: confidence scoring.} Triplets are regrouped by their
resolved, direction-corrected (domain signature, range signature) pair, and
we compute a partial-completeness-assumption (PCA) style confidence
\citep{galarraga2013amie} for each pair as its joint support over the
relation's total triplet count:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pca-confidence}
  \mathrm{conf}(r, T_d, T_r) = \frac{\mathrm{support}(r, T_d,
  T_r)}{\mathrm{total}(r)}.
\end{equation}
This confidence is thresholded into \textsc{Hard}, \textsc{Soft}, and
\textsc{Hint} constraint strengths.

\subsection{Entity-Type Cluster Verification}
\label{sec:appendix-cluster-entity-types}
Used during type canonicalization (\S\ref{sec:type-dedup}) to adjudicate a merge/split decision for one candidate cluster of raw entity-type labels, including the hyponymy (``kind of'') test discussed in \S\ref{sec:type-dedup}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for entity-type cluster merge/split verification, including the hyponymy guard.}, label={lst:cluster-entity-types}]
You are an ontology engineer. Below is a group of entity type labels that have been clustered together based on semantic similarity. Some candidates may be annotated with "(context: ...)", describing which relations they tend to appear as the subject/object of in the corpus.

Your task:
1. Decide whether ALL labels refer to the SAME conceptual type.
2. If yes: choose the BEST canonical label (most specific, most common in the field).
3. If no: split the group into sub-groups of labels that DO refer to the same type,  and for each sub-group give a canonical label.

CRITICAL TEST for step 1 -- two labels are the SAME type only if they are simply
two names for the identical concept, interchangeable in any sentence. Before
merging, apply this test: can you naturally say "an X is a kind of Y" (or "a Y,
specifically an X")? If so, they are NOT the same type -- X is a more specific
type and Y is a more general one, and they MUST be split into separate groups,
even though they are obviously related and even though they may share almost
all the same context relations. For example: "documentary film" is a kind of
"film" -> split, never merge, regardless of how similar their contexts look.
"movie" and "film" are just two names for the same thing, with no "kind of"
relationship between them -> merge. Heavy context/relation overlap is expected
and NORMAL for a specific type and its general type (a documentary film
participates in almost every relation "film" does) -- it is NOT by itself
evidence for merging; the "kind of" test always takes priority over context
similarity. When genuinely unsure whether two labels are identical or in a
"kind of" relationship, prefer splitting: a missed merge can still be corrected
later, but a wrong merge silently and permanently destroys the distinction.

Contexts remain useful evidence for the OPPOSITE case: if two labels look
similar but their contexts share no relations in common at all, prefer
splitting them into different (likely unrelated or homonymous) types.

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown), using this format:

If merging all into one type:
{"decision": "merge", "groups": [{"canonical_label": "...", "members": ["...", ...]}]}

If splitting into multiple types:
{"decision": "split", "groups": [{"canonical_label": "...", "members": ["...", ...]}, ...]}"""
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Hierarchy Placement (Pairwise Action)}
\label{sec:appendix-hierarchy-pairwise}
Used by hierarchy induction (\S\ref{sec:hierarchy-induction}) to resolve how one focal type relates to a batch of similarity-ranked candidates -- the four-way decision (\emph{parent}, \emph{child}, \emph{same\_concept}, \emph{same\_class}) plus \emph{none} described in \S\ref{sec:hierarchy-induction}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for per-node hierarchy placement decisions.}, label={lst:hierarchy-pairwise}]
You are an ontology engineer. You are given ONE new/focal type and a short list of
EXISTING candidate types. Decide how the focal type relates to AT MOST ONE of the
candidates -- if none of them relate to it in a meaningful hierarchical way, say so.

Some types are annotated with "(context: ...)", listing the canonical relations they
most often participate in -- use this as evidence, not as ground truth.

IMPORTANT: candidates (and the focal type) may themselves be abstractions formed
earlier in this same process (not raw corpus surface forms) -- treat them exactly
like any other candidate.

Choose EXACTLY ONE of these five relations:

1. "parent" -- ONE candidate is a more general type that subsumes the focal type
   (focal type IS-A candidate). Name that one candidate.
2. "child" -- the focal type is a more general type that subsumes ONE OR MORE of
   the candidates (each named candidate IS-A focal type). Use this when the focal
   type should become the parent of one or more of the listed candidates. If more
   than one candidate belongs under the focal type, name ALL of them together in
   this single response -- do not report only one and rely on a later call to
   catch the rest, since a later call may never re-examine this same candidate.
3. "same_concept" -- the focal type and ONE candidate are the IDENTICAL concept,
   just worded differently (e.g. "vehicle" / "means of transport"). Not a
   hierarchy relationship -- they should be merged into one. Name that one
   candidate.
4. "same_class" -- the focal type and ONE candidate are neither parent, child,
   nor identical, but they are plausible SIBLINGS that should share a new,
   currently-nonexistent parent (e.g. "war film" and "action film" both being
   kinds of film genre). Invent a short canonical label and a one-sentence
   definition for that new shared parent. Name that one candidate.
5. "none" -- none of the candidates have any of the above relations to the focal
   type.

RULES:
- Pick the single BEST-fitting relation, if any -- do not force a relation onto a
  candidate that's merely superficially similar.
- For every relation except "child", use "candidate": a single label string
  naming which ONE candidate it applies to (omitted/ignored for "none").
- For "child" ONLY, use "candidates": a JSON list of label strings, even if only
  one candidate applies -- e.g. "candidates": ["biographical film"] for one,
  "candidates": ["biographical film", "war film"] for two.
- "confidence" is a plain JSON number between 0.0 and 1.0 (e.g. 0.9, 0.75, 1, 0) --
  NEVER a word ("high"), a spelled-out number ("zero point nine"), or a fraction
  ("9/10").
- Only "same_class" needs "new_parent_label"/"new_parent_definition" -- omit them
  for every other relation.

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown), using one of these formats:
{{"relation": "parent", "candidate": "film", "confidence": 0.9}}
{{"relation": "child", "candidates": ["biographical film"], "confidence": 0.85}}
{{"relation": "child", "candidates": ["biographical film", "war film"], "confidence": 0.85}}
{{"relation": "same_concept", "candidate": "motion picture", "confidence": 0.9}}
{{"relation": "same_class", "candidate": "action film", "new_parent_label": "genre film", "new_parent_definition": "A film classified primarily by its genre or style.", "confidence": 0.8}}
{{"relation": "none", "confidence": 0.0}}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Entity-Name Cluster Verification}
\label{sec:appendix-cluster-entity-names}
Used during entity deduplication (\S\ref{sec:entity-dedup}) to adjudicate a merge/split decision for one candidate cluster of compound \texttt{name~[type]} entity mentions, within one parent-gated partition.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for entity-name cluster merge/split verification.}, label={lst:cluster-entity-names}]
You are a knowledge graph engineer. Below is a group of entity mentions, each in the format "name [type]". These have been clustered together based on embedding similarity.

Your task:
1. Decide which mentions refer to the SAME real-world entity.
2. Group mentions of the same entity together.
3. For each group, choose the BEST canonical name — the most complete, unambiguous form of the entity name. The canonical name must be JUST the entity name, WITHOUT the [type] bracket.
4. Each group's "members" array must contain the EXACT compound strings as provided (including the [type] part).

IMPORTANT RULES:
- Two mentions with DIFFERENT types usually refer to DIFFERENT entities (e.g., "Paris [city]" vs "Paris [person]" are two different entities).
- Two mentions with SIMILAR types may refer to the same entity (e.g., "Christopher Nolan [film director]" and "Chris Nolan [filmmaker]" are the same person).
- Abbreviations, nicknames, and partial names should be merged with their full form ONLY if the type context supports it.
- When in doubt, keep entities SEPARATE (prefer precision over recall).

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown):
{"groups": [{"canonical_label": "<just the name>", "members": ["<name [type]>", ...]}, ...]}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Relation-Direction Classification}
\label{sec:appendix-relation-direction}
Used by constraint induction's Stage~1 (\S\ref{sec:constraints}) to classify each raw surface form merged into a canonical relation as \emph{forward} or \emph{inverted} relative to that relation's own canonical direction, ahead of domain/range constraint mining.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for relation-direction (forward/inverted) classification.}, label={lst:relation-direction}]
You are an ontology engineer resolving relation directionality.

Below is a canonical relation (its chosen representative label) and the full list of raw
predicate surface forms that were merged into it during relation canonicalization. Surface forms
are merged when they denote the same underlying relationship between two entities, INCLUDING
cases where one form is the grammatical passive/inverse of another (e.g. "directed" and "was
directed by" describe the same relationship, just told with subject and object swapped).

Your task: classify EACH surface form relative to the canonical label's own direction, using
exactly one of:

1. "forward" — this surface form keeps the same subject/object roles as the canonical label.
   Example: canonical "directed", surface form "helmed" -> "forward" (same roles: person directs
   film).

2. "inverted" — this surface form swaps subject and object relative to the canonical label: what
   is the SUBJECT under the canonical label becomes the OBJECT under this surface form, and vice
   versa. Example: canonical "directed" (subject=director, object=film), surface form "was
   directed by" -> "inverted" (under this form, the film is the grammatical subject and the
   director the object).

The canonical label itself is always "forward" by definition — it is the reference direction
everything else is judged against.

Base this purely on grammatical/semantic role structure, not on which surface form is more
frequent or "sounds more natural" — frequency has nothing to do with direction.

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown), using this format:
{
  "labels": {
    "directed": "forward",
    "was directed by": "inverted",
    "helmed": "forward"
  }
}

Every surface form given to you must appear as a key in "labels", with exactly one of "forward"
or "inverted" as its value. Do not invent surface forms that were not given to you.
\end{lstlisting}
