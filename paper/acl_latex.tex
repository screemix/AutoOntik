\begin{abstract}
Graph-based retrieval-augmented generation depends on the quality of the knowledge graph (KG) it retrieves over, yet schema-free LLM-based extraction pipelines typically produce flat graphs: deduplicated entities and relations with no type hierarchy and no domain/range constraints. We present OntoDisco, a pipeline that extends schema-free KG construction with hyponymy-aware type canonicalization, a priority-ordered hierarchy induction procedure, entity deduplication gated by the induced hierarchy, and domain/range constraint mining. We evaluate OntoDisco both intrinsically, against the reference ontologies of Text2KGBench's Wikidata-TekGen and DBpedia-WebNLG subsets, and extrinsically, on downstream retrieval accuracy on the MINE benchmark. Across two LLM backbones, OntoDisco's induced types agree with the reference ontology's domain and range classes on a majority of matched relations, and where they disagree, the mismatch is overwhelmingly a matter of granularity rather than an unrelated branch of the induced hierarchy. On MINE, a knowledge graph built around OntoDisco's typed, hierarchically organized structure correctly answers a majority of retrieval queries. Beyond the pipeline itself, we propose an evaluation methodology for schema-free ontology induction that scores an induced hierarchy directly against a benchmark's own reference ontology.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds large language model (LLM) outputs in evidence retrieved at inference time \citep{lewis2020retrieval}, but retrieval over unstructured, chunked text struggles with multi-hop questions whose evidence is scattered across many chunks or documents. Graph-based RAG addresses this by retrieving over a knowledge graph (KG) instead of flat passages to support multi-hop reasoning and
% query-time generalization over specific mentions \citep{edge2024graphrag}.
% The quality of graph-based retrieval is therefore bounded by the quality of the KG that backs it: a graph with redundant, untyped nodes and unconstrained edges offers little more structure than the text it was extracted from \citep{chepurova2026wikontic}.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds large language model (LLM) outputs in evidence retrieved at inference time \citep{lewis2020retrieval}, but retrieval over unstructured, chunked text struggles with multi-hop questions whose evidence is scattered across many chunks or documents. Graph-based RAG addresses this by retrieving over a knowledge graph (KG) instead of flat passages, supporting multi-hop reasoning and query-time generalization over specific mentions rather than only exact ones \citep{edge2024graphrag}. This generalization, however, depends on the graph having actual type structure to generalize over. A graph with redundant, untyped nodes and unconstrained edges offers little more than the text it was extracted from \citep{chepurova2026wikontic}, since there is no category level above individual mentions to generalize to. Constructing that structure, a canonical, typed vocabulary organized into an is-a hierarchy with explicit domain/range constraints, and verifying that it is actually correct, without manual schema curation for every new corpus, is the problem this paper addresses.

Constructing a KG that actually supports this kind of retrieval requires
more than extracting entities and relations. It requires (i) a canonical,
deduplicated vocabulary of entity and relation surface forms, (ii) an
explicit \emph{is-a} hierarchy over entity types, so that a query about a
general category (e.g.\ \emph{artwork}) can retrieve instances of any of
its specializations without enumerating every leaf type by hand, and (iii)
relation-level domain and range constraints, so that a retrieved triple
can be validated against the schema the KG itself induces. Traditional
ontology engineering supplies all three by hand, at a cost that does not
scale to open-domain corpora. Hand-curated top-level ontologies such as
DOLCE \citep{borgo2023dolce} offer stable, philosophically motivated
top-level structure but cannot be extended automatically to an arbitrary
corpus's vocabulary.

Recent work instead automates (i) directly from text with LLMs. KGGen \citep{mo2025kggen} extracts open-vocabulary triplets and merges near-duplicate entity and relation surface forms through embedding-based clustering and LLM-adjudicated verification, producing a substantially cleaner graph than raw extraction alone. However, canonicalization in KGGen and other schema-free extraction pipelines is flat, so the resulting graph has no type hierarchy and no domain/range constraints. Without types, clustering has no symbolic signal to fall back on and must rely on embedding similarity and LLM judgment alone. This raises a concrete risk: distinct entities or relations that are surface-similar but semantically different (e.g., a film and a biography of its director) are more likely to be over-merged. A type hierarchy also gives downstream retrieval a coarser fallback unit when an exact entity or relation match is missed.

Extending flat canonicalization into a typed, hierarchical ontology introduces three problems that flat deduplication never has to face.
First, type labels are themselves open-vocabulary free text, so they need the same clustering-plus-LLM-verification treatment as entities and relations. But naively merging similar type labels tends to conflate a general category with its own specializations, for example \emph{film} and \emph{documentary film}: the two look embedding-similar even though one is strictly more specific than the other.

Second, inducing a hierarchy over the resulting canonical types is a separate problem from canonicalizing them. If placement happens in a single pass, or bottom-up, a type can get permanently attached under too general a parent. This happens whenever a more specific sibling type is only discovered later, since nothing revisits the earlier placement to correct it. Third, relation canonicalization merges surface forms without regard to grammatical role. A canonical relation can therefore silently pool a predicate together with its grammatical inverse (e.g.\ \emph{directed} and \emph{was directed by}). This fragments the domain/range evidence that constraint induction relies on, instead of reinforcing it.

We present \textbf{OntoDisco}, an ontology construction pipeline that extends schema-free approaches to close this gap: on top of a
deduplicated entity and relation vocabulary, it produces a
hyponymy-aware, hierarchically organized type vocabulary, an entity
vocabulary deduplicated against that hierarchy, and a set of
per-relation domain/range constraints mined from it.
(\S\ref{sec:methods}). Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item OntoDisco, extraction and
  canonicalization pipeline that induces a type hierarchy and
  domain/range constraints from raw text with no predefined schema
  (\S\ref{sec:methods}).
  \item An evaluation approach for schema-free ontology induction that
  scores an induced type hierarchy and its domain/range constraints
  directly against a benchmark's own reference ontology (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}).
  \item Empirical evidence that the induced type hierarchy improves
  downstream retrieval: a knowledge graph built around OntoDisco's typed,
  hierarchically organized structure correctly answers a majority of
  MINE's retrieval queries (\S\ref{sec:eval-mine}, \S\ref{sec:results-mine}).
\end{itemize}
\section{Methods: OntoDisco}
\label{sec:methods}

% \subsection{Overview}
% \label{sec:methods-overview}

OntoDisco turns a textual corpus into a canonicalized, typed knowledge graph: a deduplicated relation vocabulary, a deduplicated and hierarchically organized entity-type vocabulary, a deduplicated entity vocabulary with class assignments, and a set of relation-level domain/range constraints (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).
At first, a schema-free LLM extractor  (\S\ref{sec:extraction}) produces a collection of raw triplets: 
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G} = (s, t_s, r, o, t_o, Q),
\end{equation}
where $s$ -- subject entity, $t_s$ -- subject type, $r$ -- relation predicate, $o$ -- object entity, $t_o$ -- object type, and $Q$ -- an optional list of qualifiers extracted from texts~\citep{chepurova2026wikontic}. Then $\mathcal{G}$ is passed further for canonization and typing. Every downstream stage resolves fields into canonical identifiers, keeping each stage's data dependencies explicit and independently inspectable.

The pipeline runs ordered stages, described in
\S\ref{sec:relation-dedup}--\S\ref{sec:constraints}:  \emph{(1) Initial typed triplet extraction}, \emph{(2) relation
canonicalization}, \emph{(2) entity-type canonicalization}, which also
induces a taxonomy hierarchy over the resulting type vocabulary,
\emph{(3) entity canonicalization and class assignment}, and \emph{(4)
domain/range constraint induction}. The ordering is dictated by data
dependencies. Entity-type canonicalization is conditioned on the canonical relation vocabulary as a disambiguating signal. Entity deduplication runs after the type hierarchy because it uses the hierarchy as a symbolic gate for candidate synonyms. Constraint induction derives relation constraints directly from the extracted triplets and the outputs of the first two stages.

\label{sec:reoccurring-design}
The following design choices reoccur across stages. (1) Deduplication of synonymous surface forms is performed by clustering Contriever sentence embeddings \citep{izacard2022contriever} with HDBSCAN \citep{campello2013hdbscan} at a stage-specific cosine-similarity threshold $\theta$, and every merge/split decision is adjudicated by a single LLM call per candidate cluster. (2) Merging is done through several rounds: each round re-embeds the current pool, re-clusters it, and sends multi-member clusters to the LLM for verification, folding accepted merges back into the pool for the next round. Rounds stop once one produces no further reduction in pool size, or a fixed round cap $R$ is reached. (3) The result of each stage is checkpointed once it completes, allowing independent inspection of every stage's output and reuse in follow-up experiments. Every $\theta$ and $R$ instance below is stage-specific (relation, type, and entity deduplication each set their own); concrete values for these and every other hyperparameter introduced in \S\ref{sec:relation-dedup}--\S\ref{sec:constraints} are collected in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-hyperparams}).

\subsection{Initial Typed Triplet Extraction}
\label{sec:extraction}
Subject and object types are extracted as free text, not
constrained to any predefined schema. This schema-free design allows the canonical type vocabulary used throughout the rest of the pipeline to emerge from the data itself. The full extraction prompt is given in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompts}, Listing~\ref{lst:extraction}.

\subsection{Relation Canonicalization}
\label{sec:relation-dedup}

Relation canonicalization maps the raw, free-text \texttt{relation} strings observed across all triplets onto a canonical relation vocabulary
$\mathcal{R}^*$. Candidate clusters are generated by embedding every raw
relation surface form and clustering with HDBSCAN at similarity threshold
$\theta_r$, as described in \S\ref{sec:reoccurring-design}. Every multi-member candidate cluster is then passed to a single LLM call that decides whether to merge all members into one relation or split them into several (see the prompt in Listing~\ref{lst:cluster-relations}); this repeats for up to $R_r$ rounds until no round shrinks $\mathcal{R}^*$ any further.

\subsection{Entity Types canonicalization}
\label{sec:type-dedup}

This stage both flattens the raw type vocabulary into a canonical set
$\mathcal{T}^*$ and organizes $\mathcal{T}^*$ into a taxonomy hierarchy $\mathcal{H}^*$.

\subsubsection{Type Deduplication}

Candidate clusters over raw type labels are generated the same way as for
relations (\S\ref{sec:relation-dedup}, threshold $\theta_t$), but with one additional source of evidence: each candidate label shown to the LLM verifier is annotated with the top $k_{\text{ctx}}$ canonical relations it most often co-occurs with (a PPMI-weighted relation-argument profile), giving the verifier concrete distributional evidence beyond the bare label string.

The verification prompt additionally enforces a hyponymy (``kind of'') test: two labels are merged only if neither is a hyponym of the other, even when their relation-argument profiles overlap almost entirely. For example, \ \emph{documentary film} and \emph{film} participate in the same relations, but LLM is instructed to keep them distinct, whereas \emph{movie} and \emph{film}, true synonyms, should
be merged. The full prompt is given in Listing~\ref{lst:cluster-entity-types}. As in relation deduplication, this embed-cluster-verify cycle repeats for up to $R_t$ rounds until a round adds no further reduction to $\mathcal{T}^*$.

\subsubsection{Hierarchy induction}
\label{sec:hierarchy-induction}

Flat canonicalization removes surface redundancy but does not recover
subsumption structure. A typed knowledge graph needs an explicit is-a hierarchy over $\mathcal{T}^*$, both to gate candidate pairs during entity deduplication (\S\ref{sec:entity-dedup}) and to generalize sparse domain/range observations during constraint induction (\S\ref{sec:constraints}). We induce this hierarchy with a single
priority-ordered, top-down, pairwise placement procedure:

\paragraph{Priority queue.} Every canonical type in $\mathcal{T}^*$ is
represented by a relation-argument profile, PPMI-weighted by default. A
one-shot pass based on the Weeds precision containment measure
\citep{weeds2004characterising} computes
for every type $u$, an in-degree score counting how many other types $v$
look containable within it:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weeds-indeg}
  \mathrm{deg_{in}}(u) = \bigl| \{\, v \neq u : \mathrm{WeedsPrec}(v, u) \geq
  \tau \,\} \bigr|.
  \\
\end{equation}
$\mathrm{WeedsPrec}(v, u)$ is the fraction of $v$'s relation-argument weight that $u$
also exhibits:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{WeedsPrec}(v,u) =
  \frac{\sum_{f \in F(v)\cap F(u)} w_v(f)}
       {\sum_{f \in F(v)} w_v(f)},
\end{equation}
and $F(v)$ is the set of relation-argument features with nonzero
weight for $v$, and $\tau$ is a fixed containment threshold (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}). The in-degree is a structural signal: types are grouped into priority batches from highest in-degree to lowest to identify high-level ontology concepts first.
% If a set of external seed labels is supplied, each is matched to
% $\mathcal{T}^*$ by normalized label (or synthesized fresh if unmatched) and pinned at the top of the queue as a root candidates with no other node can be a parent of which; without seeds, the top band of the same ranking performs root discovery instead.

\paragraph{Per-node placement.} Each node is routed top-down through
any higher-priority batches have already placed: at every level, the
remaining candidates (initially the whole pool; during descent, an
already-placed node's existing children) are shown to the LLM in
similarity-ranked batches of size $B$ (the \emph{candidate batch size}; Listing~\ref{lst:hierarchy-pairwise}), and a response of ``none of these'' escalates to the next, less-similar batch, up to $E$ escalation batches (the \emph{escalation cap}). Every
batch response is one of five outcomes:
% \textbf{synonym} of a candidate, \textbf{child of} a candidate, \textbf{parent of} a candidate, \textbf{same class} as a candidate, \textbf{none} of the candidates.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{synonym} of a candidate: merge the two synonymous nodes that were not captured in flat clustering deduplication.
  \item \textbf{child of} a candidate: descend one level deeper into that
  candidate's own children at the next iteration, rather than attaching
  immediately, so that a more specific attachment is never preempted.
  \item \textbf{parent of} candidates: insert the incoming node
  \emph{above} the defined candidates, detaching its existing parent edge (if any)
  and reattaching it beneath the new node, which then settles at the
  current level.
  \item \textbf{same class} as a candidate: neither contains the other, but
  the two are plausible siblings (e.g.\ \emph{war film} and \emph{action film}, both
  film genres). A new shared parent is synthesized -- a short label and
  one-sentence definition invented on the spot -- and both the incoming node
  and the matched candidate are attached beneath it; the new parent re-enters
  the pool to be placed by a later band, the same as any other node.
  \item \textbf{none} of the candidates fit at any escalation batch: the
  node settles at its current position, as a new root if this occurred at
  the top level, or as an ordinary child of its current descent anchor
  otherwise.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:hierarchy-steps} works through all five outcomes on a small film-domain vocabulary, one placement decision per step.

\paragraph{Depth bound and guarantees.} A maximum-depth cap $D_{\max}$ bounds the worst-case number of placement decisions for any candidate node. A node that would need to exceed it is placed into a structurally separate unresolved bucket rather than being force-attached or discarded, allowing to extent the resulted hierarchy later on. The result of such procedure is always a single-parent forest at every
point in the procedure: acyclicity and the absence of transitive shortcut
edges follow directly from the construction.

\subsection{Entity Canonicalization}
\label{sec:entity-dedup}

Entity deduplication canonicalizes entity mentions and  assigns each canonical entity types. An entity's identity is the compound pair (name, canonical type) rather than name alone, so
that homonymous names typed differently (e.g.\ \emph{Paris} the city versus
\emph{Paris} the person) are never conflated by construction.

Before embedding-based comparison, candidate pairs are restricted by a symbolic gate: two mentions are only ever compared if they share
literally the same type, or are siblings under the same parent
in the induced type hierarchy (\S\ref{sec:hierarchy-induction}). HDBSCAN call only ever compares candidates within one sibling
group.

Within each partition, merging then follows the round-based procedure common to every deduplication stage (\S\ref{sec:reoccurring-design}, Listing~\ref{lst:cluster-entity-names}), applied independently per partition. Candidates are clustered at similarity threshold $\theta_e$, and each partition's embed-cluster-verify cycle repeats for up to $R_e$ rounds.

\subsection{Constraints Induction}
\label{sec:constraints}

\paragraph{Stage 0: re-resolution.} For every raw triplet, its subject and
object type strings and its relation string are looked up against the
canonical vocabularies produced by \S\ref{sec:relation-dedup} and
\S\ref{sec:type-dedup}.

\paragraph{Stage 1: relation-direction resolution.} Because relation
canonicalization merges surface forms without regard to grammatical role
(\S\ref{sec:relation-dedup}), a canonical relation can silently pool a
predicate together with its grammatical inverse (e.g.\ \emph{directed} and
\emph{was directed by}). Hence, for every canonical relation with more than one
surface form, a single LLM call classifies each surface form against the
canonical label as \emph{forward} or \emph{inverted} (Listing~\ref{lst:relation-direction}); triplets whose
original predicate was classified as inverted have their subject and object types swapped before the next stage.

\paragraph{Stage 2: hierarchy generalization.} For each canonical
relation's domain (subject-role types) and range (object-role types),
considered independently, we compute the exact lowest common ancestor (LCA) of
all observed types in the single-parent hierarchy. If the observed types span disconnected
parts of the hierarchy forest, they are partitioned by which root each
descends from and an LCA is computed independently within each partition,
yielding more than one domain (or range) signature and hence more than
one constraint for relations that genuinely connect unrelated type
families.

\paragraph{Stage 3: confidence scoring.} Triplets are regrouped by their
resolved, direction-corrected (domain signature, range signature) pair, and
we compute a partial-completeness-assumption (PCA) style confidence
\citep{galarraga2013amie} for each pair as its joint support over the
relation's total triplet count:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pca-confidence}
  \mathrm{conf}(r, T_d, T_r) = \frac{\mathrm{support}(r, T_d,
  T_r)}{\mathrm{total}(r)}.
\end{equation}
This confidence is thresholded into \textsc{Hard} ($\mathrm{conf} \geq
\tau_{\text{hard}}$), \textsc{Soft} ($\geq \tau_{\text{soft}}$), and
\textsc{Hint} ($\geq \tau_{\text{hint}}$) constraint strengths, discarded
below $\tau_{\text{hint}}$ (values in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}).

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Ontology evaluation}
\label{sec:onto-eval}
\textbf{Benchmark}. We evaluate OntoDisco on \textbf{Text2KGBench}
\citep{mihindukulasooriya2023text2kgbench}, a benchmark of sentence--triple
pairs annotated against small, hand-curated domain ontologies, comprising
two subsets: \textbf{Wikidata-TekGen} (10 domain ontologies) and
\textbf{DBpedia-WebNLG} (19 domain ontologies). Each domain ontology
defines a set of classes and object properties, every property carrying a
declared domain and range class (allowed subject and object types respectively). Every test sentence is
paired with a gold triple set $\{(s, r, o)\}$, where $r$ is drawn from the ontology's
relation vocabulary. Gold triplets carry no per-triple type annotation, so a relation's declared domain/range class stands in for the type of subject/object. We use the \texttt{test} split with 4{,}062 sentences across the 10 Wikidata-TekGen
ontologies and 2{,}014 across the 19 DBpedia-WebNLG ontologies.

% Several Wikidata-TekGen ontologies
% additionally encode a class hierarchy via \texttt{rdfs:subClassOf}, whereas
% the DBpedia-WebNLG ontologies did not.

Text2KGBench's intended task is \emph{ontology-guided} generation: a system is given the ontology and must extract compliant triplets. We consider the inverse setting: OntoDisco is schema-free and receives no ontology as input. Instead, it must induce a schema and extract triplets from the text. We then compare the induced schema and extracted triplets against the benchmark's gold ontology and gold triplets.

We used two LLM backbones driving every stage of the pipeline: Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct
\citep{qwen2025qwen3} and gpt-oss-120b \citep{openai2025gptoss}, both run with the identical pipeline hyperparameters listed in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-hyperparams}). Judging is performed in every condition by a fixed model, GPT-4o
\citep{openai2024gpt4o}, independent of whichever backbone built the KG.

% on its own, rather than only ever being evaluated one domain at a time.


\textbf{Triplet matching}. For every gold triple, we retrieve the extracted triplets OntoDisco produced from the same sentence and ask an LLM judge to select, if any, the one that expresses the same real-world fact as the gold triple, allowing for paraphrased relation wording and for subject and object being swapped (a grammatical inverse of the gold triple, e.g.\ \emph{X composed Y} vs.\ \emph{Y was composed by X}). The judge returns a matched index with direction \emph{same}, a matched index with direction \emph{inverse}, or no match. A matched extracted triple is removed from that sentence's candidate pool before the next gold triple is judged, so no single extraction can satisfy two gold triples.

Gold triples are matched from the reference side outward, specifically so that an \emph{unmatched extracted triple} is never scored as a false positive. Text2KGBench uses curated relation vocabularies of roughly 15--20 relations per domain, while a schema-free pipeline may correctly extract true facts that are simply not encoded in the gold ontology. We therefore report the \emph{recall}, defined as the proportion of gold triples that are matched, and the \emph{extra rate}, defined as the proportion of extracted triples that remain unmatched after all gold triples have been processed. The extra rate is reported separately from recall to distinguish facts extracted beyond the gold annotations from failures to recover reference triples. We also report the \emph{direction breakdown}: the proportion of matched triples classified as \emph{same} versus \emph{inverse}. This provides an indirect check of OntoDisco's relation-direction resolution
.

\textbf{{Domain/Range Type Matching}}. For every matched pair, we compare the types that OntoDisco assigned to the extracted triple's subject and object with the gold relation's declared domain and range classes. The domain and range are swapped when the match direction is \emph{inverse}. A second LLM judge classifies each side as \textsc{Exact} (synonymous types), \textsc{More General} (OntoDisco's type is a broader category that subsumes the gold type), \textsc{More Narrow} (OntoDisco's type is a subtype of the gold type), or \textsc{Not Related}. We report the resulting \emph{type-judgment distribution} over matched pairs, with domain and range sides considered independently. This distinction is important because \textsc{More General} and \textsc{More Narrow} represent different failure modes of hierarchy induction: over-flattening versus over-fragmentation.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Across intrinsic ontology evaluation (Text2KGBench, \S\ref{sec:results-text2kgbench}--\S\ref{sec:results-dbpedia}) and extrinsic retrieval evaluation (MINE, \S\ref{sec:results-mine}), OntoDisco's schema-free construction recovers a type hierarchy and canonical vocabulary that align substantially with independently-curated reference ontologies and that support effective downstream retrieval.

\subsection{Text2KGBench}
\label{sec:results-text2kgbench}

Table~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-separate} reports recall and extra rate
(\S\ref{sec:evaluation}) per domain. We measure quality only on the dataset items
whose gold triple is actually supported by its own sentence text, as
judged by an independent GPT-4o groundedness check
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-groundedness}): Text2KGBench's test sentences
are drawn from mid-article Wikipedia text without their source article's
topic, so a substantial fraction of gold subjects are established only
anaphorically, outside what the pipeline is actually shown. 

Table~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-types} reports the Averaged domain/range
type-judgment distribution (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}) over matched pairs. It measures how well the induced ontology's classes align with the
reference one. The induced type is an exact match to the gold class
51.0--57.8\% of the time; where it differs, the remainder splits between a
broader (\textsc{More General}) and a narrower (\textsc{More Narrow})
induced type rather than an unrelated one (\textsc{Not Related} stays
under 9\% on the domain side for both backbones). These results provide evidence that most
disagreement with the reference ontology is a matter of granularity, not an unrelated branch of the induced hierarchy.

\subsection{DBpedia-WebNLG}
\label{sec:results-dbpedia}

Table~\ref{tab:dbpedia-webnlg-separate} reports recall and extra rate for
both backbones on all 19 DBpedia-WebNLG domains. Unlike
Table~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-separate}, these figures are not passed
through a groundedness filter: DBpedia-WebNLG's sentences are generated
directly from the gold triples (WebNLG's own construction method) rather
than sampled from Wikipedia article bodies, so we did not perform a
groundedness check for this subset. Averaged recall is 78.8\% for
gpt-oss-120b and 76.2\% for Qwen3-Next-80B, with extra rates of 23.0\%
and 25.1\% respectively -- the two backbones perform much more similarly
here than on Wikidata-TekGen's domain/range type-judgment
(Table~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-types}), where the gap between them was
substantially wider.


\subsection{MINE}
\label{sec:results-mine}

Table~\ref{tab:mine} reports overall accuracy on MINE (\S\ref{sec:eval-mine}). OntoDisco built with gpt-oss-120b reaches 73.93\% accuracy vs KGGen 66\% with gpt4o (1{,}109/1{,}500 queries). Unlike the Text2KGBench evaluation above, MINE does not score agreement with a reference schema directly. It queries the constructed KG through embedding-similarity retrieval and a fixed-depth graph walk over entities. So this result demonstrates that the typed, canonicalized KG built for a corpus with no supplied schema improves downstream retrieval.

\paragraph{Ontology Quality.} Taken together, Tables~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-separate}--\ref{tab:mine} suggest OntoDisco's schema-free construction recovers a usable typed ontology. Domain/range disagreement with the reference ontology (Table~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-types}) is dominated by granularity mismatches (\textsc{More General}/\textsc{More Narrow}).  Unrelated types (\textsc{Not Related} stays under 9\% on the domain side and under 7\% on the range side. Together with MINE's retrieval accuracy, this is evidence that the induced hierarchy places types in approximately the right region of the ontology, and that this structure is usable for downstream retrieval, even where it does not converge on the exact reference granularity.

\section*{Limitations}

Our evaluation measures agreement between the induced ontology and a benchmark's reference schema, and the usability of the resulting KG for downstream retrieval; it does not directly measure extraction precision, since a schema-free pipeline can correctly extract facts a narrow, curated reference ontology simply does not cover, and such extractions are excluded from recall by design rather than scored as errors. Finally, our evaluation covers English-language corpora and existing benchmark ontologies only; generalization to other languages remains untested.

\section{Sentence Groundedness Analysis}
\label{sec:appendix-groundedness}

Text2KGBench's Wikidata-TekGen sentences are drawn from mid-article Wikipedia text without their source article's title or topic, while its gold triplets are annotated against the full article. The underlying corpus's own construction methodology \citep{agarwal2021tekgen} aligns a Wikidata triple to a sentence by requiring only the \emph{object} entity's alias to occur in it; when the \emph{subject} is absent, a single third-person pronoun is substituted with the subject's canonical name -- but this does not cover zero-anaphora (topic continuity with no pronoun to replace at all), which is the pattern we observe empirically in most cases. To our knowledge this has not been previously quantified specifically for Text2KGBench.
%This class of noise is well documented for distant supervision in relation extraction generally \citep{mintz2009distant, riedel2010modeling}, and DocRED \citep{yao2019docred} reports an analogous statistic built to motivate the opposite fix (document-level rather than sentence-level extraction): at least 40.7\% of its relational facts require reading multiple sentences. 
% though it is consistent with the known limitations of the distant-supervision alignment its underlying corpus uses.

We use an additional GPT-4o judge to classify, per gold triple, whether its sentence alone -- no outside knowledge, no article context -- actually supports it: the subject must be identifiable within the sentence (allowing name-form variation, e.g. ``Charles Spurgeon Johnson'' in the sentence vs.\ ``Charles S. Johnson'' in the reference still counts as grounded), and the object and relationship must actually be stated or implied. Recall in \S\ref{sec:results-text2kgbench} is reported with flagged (ungrounded) triplets excluded from the denominator, as a post-filter over the already-collected judge results requiring no re-extraction or re-matching.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for typed triplet extraction.}, label={lst:extraction}]
You are an algorithm designed to extract structured knowledge from texts to build a Wikidata-like knowledge graph. A knowledge graph consists of **triplets** in the format (subject, relation, object), where:

- **Subject**: A named entity or a concept that describes a group of people, events, or any abstract objects that serves as the source of the relation.
- **Relation**: A Wikidata-style predicate that connects the subject and object.
- **Object**: A named entity or a concept that describes a group of people, events, or any abstract objects that is related to the subject.

Additionally, some triplets may have **qualifiers** that provide more context (e.g., date, place, or other attributes). Qualifiers should have relations and object like triplets do, but instead of subject their relation connects an object and the triplet qualifier belongs to. **Qualifiers must always be attached to a triplet** and never exist as standalone triplets.

You will receive a text labeled **"Text:"**. Your task is to extract meaningful triplets that represent factual relationships.

### Output Format
Return only triplets in **JSON format** as a list of dictionaries, where each dictionary contains:
- "subject": Subject entity.
- "relation": Relation connecting subject and object.
- "object": Object entity.
-  "qualifiers": List of dictionaries, where each dictionary contains:
    - "relation": Relation connecting triplet and object,
    - "object": Object entity connected to the main triplet
- "subject_type": a class that describes the subject
- "object_type": a class that describes the object

NO additional text, NO "```" in json, return ONLY triplets in JSON format.

\subsection{Entity-Type Cluster Verification}
\label{sec:appendix-cluster-entity-types}
Used during type canonicalization (\S\ref{sec:type-dedup}) to adjudicate a merge/split decision for one candidate cluster of raw entity-type labels, including the hyponymy (``kind of'') test discussed in \S\ref{sec:type-dedup}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for entity-type cluster merge/split verification, including the hyponymy guard.}, label={lst:cluster-entity-types}]
You are an ontology engineer. Below is a group of entity type labels that have been clustered together based on semantic similarity. Some candidates may be annotated with "(context: ...)", describing which relations they tend to appear as the subject/object of in the corpus.

Your task:
1. Decide whether ALL labels refer to the SAME conceptual type.
2. If yes: choose the BEST canonical label (most specific, most common in the field).
3. If no: split the group into sub-groups of labels that DO refer to the same type,  and for each sub-group give a canonical label.

CRITICAL TEST for step 1 -- two labels are the SAME type only if they are simply
two names for the identical concept, interchangeable in any sentence. Before
merging, apply this test: can you naturally say "an X is a kind of Y" (or "a Y,
specifically an X")? If so, they are NOT the same type -- X is a more specific
type and Y is a more general one, and they MUST be split into separate groups,
even though they are obviously related and even though they may share almost
all the same context relations. For example: "documentary film" is a kind of
"film" -> split, never merge, regardless of how similar their contexts look.
"movie" and "film" are just two names for the same thing, with no "kind of"
relationship between them -> merge. Heavy context/relation overlap is expected
and NORMAL for a specific type and its general type (a documentary film
participates in almost every relation "film" does) -- it is NOT by itself
evidence for merging; the "kind of" test always takes priority over context
similarity. When genuinely unsure whether two labels are identical or in a
"kind of" relationship, prefer splitting: a missed merge can still be corrected
later, but a wrong merge silently and permanently destroys the distinction.

Contexts remain useful evidence for the OPPOSITE case: if two labels look
similar but their contexts share no relations in common at all, prefer
splitting them into different (likely unrelated or homonymous) types.

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown), using this format:

If merging all into one type:
{"decision": "merge", "groups": [{"canonical_label": "...", "members": ["...", ...]}]}

If splitting into multiple types:
{"decision": "split", "groups": [{"canonical_label": "...", "members": ["...", ...]}, ...]}"""
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Hierarchy Placement (Pairwise Action)}
\label{sec:appendix-hierarchy-pairwise}
Used by hierarchy induction (\S\ref{sec:hierarchy-induction}) to resolve how one focal type relates to a batch of similarity-ranked candidates -- the four-way decision (\emph{parent}, \emph{child}, \emph{same\_concept}, \emph{same\_class}) plus \emph{none} described in \S\ref{sec:hierarchy-induction}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for per-node hierarchy placement decisions.}, label={lst:hierarchy-pairwise}]
You are an ontology engineer. You are given ONE new/focal type and a short list of
EXISTING candidate types. Decide how the focal type relates to AT MOST ONE of the
candidates -- if none of them relate to it in a meaningful hierarchical way, say so.

Some types are annotated with "(context: ...)", listing the canonical relations they
most often participate in -- use this as evidence, not as ground truth.

IMPORTANT: candidates (and the focal type) may themselves be abstractions formed
earlier in this same process (not raw corpus surface forms) -- treat them exactly
like any other candidate.

Choose EXACTLY ONE of these five relations:

1. "parent" -- ONE candidate is a more general type that subsumes the focal type
   (focal type IS-A candidate). Name that one candidate.
2. "child" -- the focal type is a more general type that subsumes ONE OR MORE of
   the candidates (each named candidate IS-A focal type). Use this when the focal
   type should become the parent of one or more of the listed candidates. If more
   than one candidate belongs under the focal type, name ALL of them together in
   this single response -- do not report only one and rely on a later call to
   catch the rest, since a later call may never re-examine this same candidate.
3. "same_concept" -- the focal type and ONE candidate are the IDENTICAL concept,
   just worded differently (e.g. "vehicle" / "means of transport"). Not a
   hierarchy relationship -- they should be merged into one. Name that one
   candidate.
4. "same_class" -- the focal type and ONE candidate are neither parent, child,
   nor identical, but they are plausible SIBLINGS that should share a new,
   currently-nonexistent parent (e.g. "war film" and "action film" both being
   kinds of film genre). Invent a short canonical label and a one-sentence
   definition for that new shared parent. Name that one candidate.
5. "none" -- none of the candidates have any of the above relations to the focal
   type.

RULES:
- Pick the single BEST-fitting relation, if any -- do not force a relation onto a
  candidate that's merely superficially similar.
- For every relation except "child", use "candidate": a single label string
  naming which ONE candidate it applies to (omitted/ignored for "none").
- For "child" ONLY, use "candidates": a JSON list of label strings, even if only
  one candidate applies -- e.g. "candidates": ["biographical film"] for one,
  "candidates": ["biographical film", "war film"] for two.
- "confidence" is a plain JSON number between 0.0 and 1.0 (e.g. 0.9, 0.75, 1, 0) --
  NEVER a word ("high"), a spelled-out number ("zero point nine"), or a fraction
  ("9/10").
- Only "same_class" needs "new_parent_label"/"new_parent_definition" -- omit them
  for every other relation.

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown), using one of these formats:
{"relation": "parent", "candidate": "film", "confidence": 0.9}
{"relation": "child", "candidates": ["biographical film"], "confidence": 0.85}
{"relation": "child", "candidates": ["biographical film", "war film"], "confidence": 0.85}
{"relation": "same_concept", "candidate": "motion picture", "confidence": 0.9}
{"relation": "same_class", "candidate": "action film", "new_parent_label": "genre film", "new_parent_definition": "A film classified primarily by its genre or style.", "confidence": 0.8}
{"relation": "none", "confidence": 0.0}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Entity-Name Cluster Verification}
\label{sec:appendix-cluster-entity-names}
Used during entity deduplication (\S\ref{sec:entity-dedup}) to adjudicate a merge/split decision for one candidate cluster of compound \texttt{name~[type]} entity mentions, within one parent-gated partition.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for entity-name cluster merge/split verification.}, label={lst:cluster-entity-names}]
You are a knowledge graph engineer. Below is a group of entity mentions, each in the format "name [type]". These have been clustered together based on embedding similarity.

Your task:
1. Decide which mentions refer to the SAME real-world entity.
2. Group mentions of the same entity together.
3. For each group, choose the BEST canonical name -- the most complete, unambiguous form of the entity name. The canonical name must be JUST the entity name, WITHOUT the [type] bracket.
4. Each group's "members" array must contain the EXACT compound strings as provided (including the [type] part).

IMPORTANT RULES:
- Two mentions with DIFFERENT types usually refer to DIFFERENT entities (e.g., "Paris [city]" vs "Paris [person]" are two different entities).
- Two mentions with SIMILAR types may refer to the same entity (e.g., "Christopher Nolan [film director]" and "Chris Nolan [filmmaker]" are the same person).
- Abbreviations, nicknames, and partial names should be merged with their full form ONLY if the type context supports it.
- When in doubt, keep entities SEPARATE (prefer precision over recall).

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown):
{"groups": [{"canonical_label": "<just the name>", "members": ["<name [type]>", ...]}, ...]}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Relation-Direction Classification}
\label{sec:appendix-relation-direction}
Used by constraint induction's Stage~1 (\S\ref{sec:constraints}) to classify each raw surface form merged into a canonical relation as \emph{forward} or \emph{inverted} relative to that relation's own canonical direction, ahead of domain/range constraint mining.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for relation-direction (forward/inverted) classification.}, label={lst:relation-direction}]
You are an ontology engineer resolving relation directionality.

Below is a canonical relation (its chosen representative label) and the full list of raw
predicate surface forms that were merged into it during relation canonicalization. Surface forms
are merged when they denote the same underlying relationship between two entities, INCLUDING
cases where one form is the grammatical passive/inverse of another (e.g. "directed" and "was
directed by" describe the same relationship, just told with subject and object swapped).

Your task: classify EACH surface form relative to the canonical label's own direction, using
exactly one of:

1. "forward" -- this surface form keeps the same subject/object roles as the canonical label.
   Example: canonical "directed", surface form "helmed" -> "forward" (same roles: person directs
   film).

2. "inverted" -- this surface form swaps subject and object relative to the canonical label: what
   is the SUBJECT under the canonical label becomes the OBJECT under this surface form, and vice
   versa. Example: canonical "directed" (subject=director, object=film), surface form "was
   directed by" -> "inverted" (under this form, the film is the grammatical subject and the
   director the object).

The canonical label itself is always "forward" by definition -- it is the reference direction
everything else is judged against.

Base this purely on grammatical/semantic role structure, not on which surface form is more
frequent or "sounds more natural" -- frequency has nothing to do with direction.

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown), using this format:
{
  "labels": {
    "directed": "forward",
    "was directed by": "inverted",
    "helmed": "forward"
  }
}

Every surface form given to you must appear as a key in "labels", with exactly one of "forward"
or "inverted" as its value. Do not invent surface forms that were not given to you.
\end{lstlisting}
