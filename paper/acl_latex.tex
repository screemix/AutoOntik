\begin{abstract}
Graph-based retrieval-augmented generation depends on the quality of the knowledge graph (KG) it retrieves over, yet schema-free LLM-based extraction pipelines typically produce flat graphs: deduplicated entities and relations with no type hierarchy and no domain/range constraints.
% We present AutoOntic, a pipeline that extends schema-free KG construction with hyponymy-aware type canonicalization, a priority-ordered hierarchy induction procedure, entity deduplication gated by the induced hierarchy, and domain/range constraint mining.
We present AutoOntic, a pipeline that automatically constructs an ontology from text by canonicalizing open-vocabulary entity types and relations, inducing a hierarchy over the canonicalized types, and mining relation-level domain and range constraints. Its hierarchy induction procedure prioritizes types using corpus-derived relation-argument profiles and places them through local, LLM-adjudicated parent/child checks, allowing newly inserted types to trigger reassignment of existing parent-child links. The resulting hierarchy constrains entity deduplication and supports the induction of relation-level domain/range constraints.
% We evaluate AutoOntic both intrinsically, against the reference ontologies of Text2KGBench's Wikidata-TekGen and DBpedia-WebNLG subsets, and extrinsically, on downstream retrieval accuracy on the MINE benchmark. Across two LLM backbones, AutoOntic's induced types agree with the reference ontology's domain and range classes on a majority of matched relations, and where they disagree, the mismatch is a matter of granularity rather than an unrelated branch of the induced hierarchy. On MINE, a knowledge graph built around AutoOntic's typed, hierarchically organized structure correctly answers a majority of retrieval queries.
% We evaluate AutoOntic both intrinsically, against the reference ontologies of Text2KGBench's Wikidata-TekGen and DBpedia-WebNLG subsets, and extrinsically, on downstream retrieval accuracy on the MINE benchmark. Across two LLM backbones, over 90\% of AutoOntic's induced endpoint types on matched triples are semantically aligned with the reference domain and range classes: about half match exactly, while most remaining differences concern granularity rather than unrelated types. On MINE, knowledge graphs organized using an ontology built by AutoOntic correctly answer 73.93\% of retrieval queries.
% Beyond the pipeline itself, we propose an evaluation methodology for schema-free ontology induction that scores an induced hierarchy directly against a benchmark's own reference ontology.
For intrinsic evaluation, we introduce a
reference-based protocol that classifies induced ontology types as exact, broader, narrower, or unrelated to reference domain and range classes. On the Wikidata-TekGen and DBpedia-WebNLG subsets of Text2KGBench, over 90\% of AutoOntic's induced endpoint types are semantically aligned with the reference classes across two LLM backbones: about half match exactly, while most remaining differences concern granularity. On MINE, knowledge graphs organized using an ontology built by AutoOntic achieve 73.93\% retrieval accuracy.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation grounds large language model (LLM) outputs in retrieved evidence \citep{lewis2020retrieval}. However, retrieval over chunked text can struggle when answering multi-hop questions that require connecting evidence across documents. Graph-based approaches organize this evidence into knowledge graphs (KGs), where information is represented as entities and relations, enabling retrieval across connected facts \citep{edge2024graphrag}. The effectiveness of this approach depends not only on which facts a knowledge graph (KG) contains, but also on how those facts are organized. In particular, KG-RAG benefits from ontology \citep{chepurova2026wikontic}, a hierarchy of entity types that represent general categories and their specializations, and relation domain and range constraints that express which types of entities a relation connects. These structures provide a basis for category-level retrieval and structural validation, but must be constructed reliably to be useful.

Building a KG with this structure from text remains challenging. Schema-free extraction methods such as KGGen \citep{mo2025kggen} extract and canonicalize entities and relations to provide more compact and well-connected KG without requiring a predefined domain schema, but do not explicitly induce a hierarchy of entity types or relation domain and range constraints. Conversely, ontology-aware KG construction methods can use pre-built ontologies to organize and constrain extracted knowledge \citep{chepurova2026wikontic}, but applying an existing ontology to a new corpus may require aligning its vocabulary with corpus-specific expressions and introducing concepts absent from the original schema. These observations motivate a method that goes beyond flat KG construction to induce a corpus-grounded ontology, while retaining the option to incorporate an existing ontology when available.


We present \textbf{AutoOntic}, an ontology construction pipeline that extends schema-free approaches to close this gap: on top of a deduplicated entity and relation vocabulary, it produces a
hyponymy-aware, hierarchically organized type vocabulary, an entity
vocabulary deduplicated against that hierarchy, and a set of
per-relation domain/range constraints mined from it. The system can also extend an existing ontology when one is provided.
(\S\ref{sec:methods}). Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item AutoOntic, a schema-free ontology induction method that combines corpus-derived relation-argument profiles with priority-ordered, top-down type placement. Unlike flat canonicalization, the procedure explicitly tests subsumption relationships and can retroactively reassign parent-child links when newly inserted types reveal a more specific placement. The resulting hierarchy is then used to constrain entity deduplication and to generalize observed relation domain/range types.
  \item An evaluation approach for schema-free ontology induction that scores an induced type hierarchy and its domain/range constraints directly against a benchmark's own reference ontology, classifying each type mismatch as \textsc{Exact}, \textsc{More General}, or \textsc{More Narrow} rather than a flat right/wrong (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}).
  \item Empirical evidence that the induced type hierarchy improves downstream retrieval: over 90\% of AutoOntic's induced types are semantically aligned with the reference classes, and on MINE, knowledge graphs organized around AutoOntic's typed, hierarchically organized structure achieve 73.93\% retrieval accuracy, correctly answering a majority of MINE's retrieval queries (\S\ref{sec:eval-mine}, \S\ref{sec:results-mine}).
\end{itemize}
\section{Methods: AutoOntic}
\label{sec:methods}

% \subsection{Overview}
% \label{sec:methods-overview}

AutoOntic turns a textual corpus into a canonicalized, typed knowledge graph: a deduplicated relation vocabulary, a deduplicated and hierarchically organized entity-type vocabulary, a deduplicated entity vocabulary with class assignments, and a set of relation-level domain/range constraints (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).
At first, a schema-free LLM extractor  (\S\ref{sec:extraction}) produces a collection $\mathcal{G}$ of raw triplets,
each represented as:
\begin{equation}
g = (s, t_s, r, o, t_o, Q),
\end{equation}
where $s$ -- subject entity, $t_s$ -- subject type, $r$ -- relation predicate, $o$ -- object entity, $t_o$ -- object type, and $Q$ -- an optional list of qualifiers extracted from texts~\citep{chepurova2026wikontic}. Then $\mathcal{G}$ is passed further for canonicalization and typing. Every downstream stage resolves fields into canonical identifiers, keeping each stage's data dependencies explicit and independently inspectable.

% \S\ref{sec:relation-dedup}--\S\ref{sec:constraints}:  \emph{(1) Initial typed triplet extraction}, \emph{(2) relation
% canonicalization}, \emph{(3) entity-type canonicalization}, which also
% induces a taxonomy hierarchy over the resulting type vocabulary,
% \emph{(4) entity class assignment and canonicalization}, and \emph{(5)
% domain/range constraint induction}. The ordering is dictated by data
% dependencies. Entity-type canonicalization is conditioned on the canonical relation vocabulary as a disambiguating signal. Entity deduplication runs after the type hierarchy because it uses the hierarchy as a symbolic gate for candidate synonyms. Constraint induction derives relation constraints directly from the extracted triplets and the outputs of the first two stages.

The AutoOntic pipeline comprises six ordered stages:
\emph{(1) typed triplet extraction, (2) relation canonicalization,
(3) entity-type canonicalization, (4) hierarchy induction,
(5) entity canonicalization and class assignment, and
(6) domain/range constraint induction}.
This ordering follows data dependencies: canonical relations inform
type-label disambiguation; the induced type hierarchy constrains
candidate entity merges; and the canonical vocabularies and
direction-corrected triplets provide evidence for domain/range constraints.

\paragraph{Shared canonicalization procedure.}
\label{sec:reoccurring-design}
The following design choices reoccur across stages. (1) Deduplication of synonymous surface forms is performed by clustering Contriever sentence embeddings \citep{izacard2022contriever} with HDBSCAN \citep{campello2013hdbscan} at a stage-specific cosine-similarity threshold $\theta$, and every merge/split decision is adjudicated by a single LLM call per candidate cluster. (2) Merging is done through several rounds: each round re-embeds the current pool, re-clusters it, and sends multi-member clusters to the LLM for verification, folding accepted merges back into the pool for the next round. The procedure repeats until the vocabulary size stops reducing or a configurable round limit $R$ is reached. (3) The result of each stage is checkpointed once it completes, allowing independent inspection of every stage's output and reuse in follow-up experiments. Every $\theta$ and $R$ instance below is stage-specific (relation, type, and entity deduplication each set their own); concrete values for these and every other hyperparameter introduced in \S\ref{sec:relation-dedup}--\S\ref{sec:constraints} are collected in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-hyperparams}).

\subsection{Typed Triplet Extraction}
\label{sec:extraction}
An LLM extracts subject--relation--object triplets enriched with subject/object types and contextual qualifiers with meta-information that cannot be expressed as a standalone triplet such as time, location, or condition. Types are initially unconstrained free text. Each extracted record has the form $g=(s,t_s,r,o,t_o,Q)$, where $s,o$ are entity mentions, $t_s,t_o$ their raw types, $r$ the relation, and $Q$ an optional qualifier list. The extracted records form $\mathcal{G}$. The full extraction prompt is given in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompts}, Listing~\ref{lst:extraction}.

\subsection{Relation Canonicalization}
\label{sec:relation-dedup}

Relation canonicalization maps the raw, free-text \texttt{relation} strings observed across all triplets onto a canonical relation vocabulary
$\mathcal{R}^*$. Candidate clusters are generated by embedding every raw
relation surface form and clustering with HDBSCAN at similarity threshold
$\theta_r$, as described in \S\ref{sec:reoccurring-design}. Every multi-member candidate cluster is then passed to a single LLM call that decides whether to merge all members into one relation or split them into several (see the prompt in Listing~\ref{lst:cluster-relations}); this repeats for up to $R_r$ rounds until no round shrinks $\mathcal{R}^*$ any further.

\subsection{Entity-Type Canonicalization}
\label{sec:type-dedup}

Raw type labels are canonicalized into $\mathcal{T}^*$.
Canonical types are then organized into a single-parent hierarchy
$\mathcal{H}^*$, which may form a forest.
Here, entity types correspond to ontology classes.

\paragraph{Type Deduplication}
Candidate clusters over raw type labels are generated the same way as for
relations (\S\ref{sec:relation-dedup}, threshold $\theta_t$), but with one additional source of evidence: each candidate label shown to the LLM verifier is annotated with the top $k_{\text{ctx}}$ canonical relations it most often co-occurs with (a PPMI-weighted relation-argument profile). These profiles provide corpus-derived structural evidence beyond lexical or embedding similarity: two types may have similar labels but different relational roles, or different labels but nearly identical distributions of relational contexts. The verification step uses this evidence alongside the labels to decide whether a candidate pair should be merged.

The verification prompt additionally enforces a hyponymy (``kind of'') test: two labels are merged only if neither is a hyponym of the other, even when they are lexically similar or their relation-argument profiles overlap almost entirely. For example, \ \emph{documentary film} and \emph{film} are similar and participate in the same relations, but LLM is instructed to keep them distinct, whereas \emph{movie} and \emph{film}, true synonyms, should
be merged. The full prompt is given in Listing~\ref{lst:cluster-entity-types}. As in relation deduplication, this embed-cluster-verify cycle repeats for up to $R_t$ rounds until a round adds no further reduction to $\mathcal{T}^*$.

\subsection{Hierarchy Induction}
\label{sec:hierarchy-induction}
Flat type canonicalization removes surface redundancy but does not encode subsumption relationships between them. AutoOntic therefore induces an explicit is-a hierarchy over $\mathcal{T}^*$ to support two operations that depend on these relationships: restricting entity-merge candidates to structurally compatible types
(\S\ref{sec:entity-dedup}) and generalizing observed domain/range types to shared ancestors when estimating relation constraints (\S\ref{sec:constraints}).

In AutoOntic, canonical types are organized into a single-parent hierarchy $\mathcal{H}^*$, which may form a forest. Rather than asking an LLM to organize the entire type vocabulary in a single call, AutoOntic decomposes placement into local decisions over the current hierarchy $\mathcal{H}^*$, inserting types one by one from general to specific. To find such ordering of entity types, we compare the PPMI-weighted relation-argument profiles associated with each deduplicated type. Following the distributional inclusion principle, a more general type is expected to occur in a broader range of relational contexts than its more specific subtypes. We use Weeds precision \citep{weeds2004characterising} to estimate the extent to which the contexts associated with one type are contained in those associated with another. The resulting containment scores define an approximate ordering for hierarchy construction; they are not treated as proof of subsumption. Actual parent-child relationships are determined by the subsequent LLM-adjudicated placement procedure, which can revise earlier attachments as additional types are inserted.

% Types are inserted into the $\mathcal{H*}$ one by one from general to specific. The hierarchy can be initialized from a seed or existing ontology and extended with types discovered in new text. External ontologies can be used as seeds for the $\mathcal{H*}$, so AutoOntic can complement existing ontology. To place an entity type, AutoOntic traverses the currently built hierarchy $\mathcal{H*}$ downward from the current roots. At each level, the LLM performs separate \textbf{parent} and, if no parent is found, \textbf{child} checks. A parent match continues traversal through that node's children. A child match attaches the matched nodes beneath the new type, reattaching ancestors that would create cycles, and stops traversal. If neither succeeds, the type is inserted at the current position. When a level contains more than $B$ candidates, a consolidation call attaches clear siblings under synthesized parents proposed by the LLM. The prompt for parent/child check, and consolidation calls are presented in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-hierarchy-pairwise}.

Starting from the roots, the procedure checks whether the new type belongs below an existing node or should instead become a parent of one or more existing nodes. A \textbf{parent} match directs the traversal into that node's subtree; a \textbf{child} match triggers insertion and reassignment of the affected parent-child links, \textbf{synonym} match merge two synonymous nodes that are not in hyponymy relation. If neither relation holds, the type is placed at the current position. When a level contains too many candidates for a single comparison, a \textbf{consolidation} LLM call groups clear siblings under a proposed parent.

This design keeps each decision focused on a bounded part of the hierarchy, reducing the context required for an individual LLM call and avoiding repeated presentation of the entire ontology. More importantly, the decisions have explicit structural consequences: a later, more general type can correct an earlier attachment by becoming the parent of an existing node. Thus, the procedure does not merely append labels to a taxonomy; it incrementally constructs and repairs the hierarchy as new evidence becomes available. The local checks, consolidation procedure, and reassignment rules are specified in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompts}.

\paragraph{Depth bound and guarantees.} A maximum-depth cap $D_{\max}$ bounds the worst-case number of placement decisions for any candidate node. A node that would need to exceed it is placed into a structurally separate unresolved bucket rather than being force-attached or discarded, allowing to extent the resulted hierarchy later on. The result of such procedure is always a single-parent forest at every
point in the procedure: acyclicity and the absence of transitive shortcut
edges follow directly from the construction.

\subsection{Entity Canonicalization}
\label{sec:entity-dedup}

Entity canonicalization step assigns each entity its canonical type  and deduplicates synonymous mentions. An entity's identity is the compound pair (name, canonical type) rather than name alone, so
that homonymous names typed differently (e.g.\ \emph{Paris} the city versus \emph{Paris} the person) are never conflated by construction.

Before embedding-based comparison, candidate pairs are restricted by a symbolic gate: two mentions are only ever compared if they share
literally the same type, or are siblings under the same parent
in the induced type hierarchy (\S\ref{sec:hierarchy-induction}). Clustering entities surface forms is performed with HDBSCAN  among candidates within one sibling
group. Within each such partition, merging then follows the round-based procedure common to every deduplication stage (\S\ref{sec:reoccurring-design}, Listing~\ref{lst:cluster-entity-names}), applied independently per partition. Candidates are clustered at similarity threshold $\theta_e$, and each partition's embed-cluster-verify cycle repeats for up to $R_e$ rounds.

Constraint induction first re-resolves every raw triplet against the canonical relation and type vocabularies, $\mathcal{R*}$ and $\mathcal{T*}$, produced by \S\ref{sec:relation-dedup} and \S\ref{sec:type-dedup}. Because relation canonicalization may merge a predicate with its grammatical inverse (e.g.\ \emph{directed} and \emph{was directed by}), each canonical relation with multiple surface forms is processed by an LLM that classifies each form as \emph{forward} or \emph{inverted}; inverted forms then have their subject and object types swapped before constraint estimation.

For each canonical relation's domain (subject-role types) and range (object-role types),
considered independently. We compute the exact lowest common ancestor (LCA) of observed subect/object types in the single-parent hierarchy. If observations span disconnected roots, they are partitioned by root and an LCA is computed within each partition, yielding multiple domain/range signatures when a relation connects unrelated type families.

For each canonical relation, domain and range types are considered independently. We compute the exact lowest common ancestor (LCA) of observed types in $\mathcal{H}^*$. If observations span disconnected roots, they are partitioned by root and an LCA is computed within each partition, yielding multiple domain/range signatures when a relation connects unrelated type families.

Finally, triplets are grouped by their direction-corrected $(T_d,T_r)$ signature, and each signature is scored by a confidence equal to the share of relation $r$'s own triplets it accounts for:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{conf}(r,T_d,T_r)=
\frac{\mathrm{support}(r,T_d,T_r)}{\mathrm{total}(r)},
\end{equation}
where $T_d, T_r$ - entity types of domain and range correspondingly.

\subsection{Ontology evaluation}
\label{sec:onto-eval}
\textbf{Benchmark}. We evaluate AutoOntic on \textbf{Text2KGBench}
\citep{mihindukulasooriya2023text2kgbench}, a benchmark of sentence--triple
pairs annotated against small, hand-curated domain ontologies, comprising
two subsets: \textbf{Wikidata-TekGen} (10 domain ontologies) and
\textbf{DBpedia-WebNLG} (19 domain ontologies). Each domain ontology
defines a set of relations coupled with domain and range types (allowed subject and object types respectively). Every test sentence is
paired with a gold triple set $\{(s, r, o)\}$, where $r$ is drawn from the ontology's
relation vocabulary. We use the \texttt{test} split with 4{,}062 sentences across the 10 Wikidata-TekGen ontologies and 2{,}014 across the 19 DBpedia-WebNLG ontologies. 
% Gold triplets carry no per-triple type annotation, so a relation's declared domain/range class stands in for the type of subject/object. 

% Several Wikidata-TekGen ontologies
% additionally encode a class hierarchy via \texttt{rdfs:subClassOf}, whereas
% the DBpedia-WebNLG ontologies did not.

Text2KGBench's intended task is \emph{ontology-guided} generation: a system is given the ontology and must extract compliant triplets. We consider the inverse setting: AutoOntic is schema-free and receives no ontology as input. Instead, it must induce a schema and extract triplets from the text. We then compare the induced schema and extracted triplets against the benchmark's gold ontology and gold triplets.

We used two LLM backbones driving every stage of the pipeline: Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct
\citep{qwen2025qwen3} and gpt-oss-120b \citep{openai2025gptoss}, both run with the identical pipeline hyperparameters listed in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-hyperparams}). Judging is performed in every condition by a fixed model, GPT-4o
\citep{openai2024gpt4o}, independent of whichever backbone built the KG.

\textbf{Triple matching.} For each gold triple, we collect the triples extracted by AutoOntic from the same sentence and ask an LLM judge to identify an extracted triple that expresses the same underlying fact, allowing for paraphrased relation labels and inverse formulations that swap the subject and object (e.g., \emph{X composed Y} vs.\ \emph{Y was composed by X}). The judge returns the index of a matching triple and labels its direction as \emph{same} or \emph{inverse}, or indicates that no match exists. Matching is one-to-one: once an extracted triple is matched to a gold triple, it is removed from the candidate pool and cannot be matched to another gold triple.

We match triples from the gold side outward to evaluate recovery of the reference annotations without automatically treating every unmatched extraction as an error. Text2KGBench uses curated relation vocabularies containing roughly 15--20 relations per domain, whereas a schema-free extraction system may recover valid facts that are not represented in the gold annotations. We therefore report three complementary metrics. \textbf{Recall} is the proportion of gold triples matched by extracted triples. The \textbf{extra rate} is the proportion of extracted triples left unmatched after all gold triples have been processed; these triples may represent facts omitted from the reference annotations rather than extraction errors. We report the extra rate separately from recall because unmatched extractions cannot be reliably classified as incorrect solely on the basis of their absence from the gold annotations.
% Finally, the \textbf{direction breakdown} reports the proportions of matched triples classified as \emph{same} and \emph{inverse}, providing a diagnostic of relation-direction consistency. 

\textbf{Domain/Range Type Matching.} For each matched triple, we compare AutoOntic's subject and object types with the gold relation's domain and range classes. For matches classified as \emph{inverse}, we swap the gold domain and range. An LLM judge classifies each comparison as \textsc{Exact} (equivalent types), \textsc{More General} (AutoOntic's type subsumes the gold type), \textsc{More Narrow} (AutoOntic's type is a subtype of the gold type), or \textsc{Not Related}. We report the distribution of these judgments separately for domain and range, thereby distinguishing exact type alignment in triplets from differences in the generality of the induced types.

\textbf{Constraint Plausibility.} Type matching evaluates individual triples, whereas AutoOntic also induces relation-level domain and range constraints by generalizing observed argument types using their least common ancestor (LCA). To evaluate these constraints, for each matched triple we identify the induced constraint(s) whose domain and range cover the triple's subject and object types, accounting for inverse matches and allowing multiple constraints when a relation spans disconnected hierarchy partitions. We then compare each selected constraint's domain and range with the corresponding gold classes using the same four judgment categories as in Domain/Range Type Matching. We report the resulting distributions alongside each constraint's support and confidence, including constraints below the $\tau_{\text{hint}}$ threshold and regardless of their \textsc{Hard}, \textsc{Soft}, or \textsc{Hint} tier (\S\ref{sec:constraints}). Comparing instance-level type judgments with constraint-level judgments shows how well the induced constraints align with the reference ontology and whether they tend to be more general or more specific than the gold classes.

Text2KGBench's Ontology Conformance metric measures whether extracted triples use relations from the input ontology's canonical relation vocabulary; eaving domain/range validation as a direction for future extension\citep{mihindukulasooriya2023text2kgbench}. Our evaluation complements this metric by assessing domain and range alignment at both the instance and induced-constraint levels, using graded judgments that distinguish equivalent, broader, narrower, and unrelated types.

\subsection{Text2KGBench}
\label{sec:results-text2kgbench}

Tables~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-separate}--\ref{tab:dbpedia-webnlg-separate} report recall and extra rate
(\S\ref{sec:evaluation}) per domain. For Wikidata-TekGen subset, we measure quality only on the dataset items
whose gold triple is actually supported by its own sentence text, as
judged by an independent GPT-4o groundedness check
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-groundedness}): Text2KGBench's test sentences
are drawn from mid-article Wikipedia text without their source article's
topic, so a substantial fraction of gold subjects are established only
anaphorically, outside what the pipeline is actually shown. 

Table~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-types} reports the domain/range
type-judgment distribution (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}) over matched pairs, separately for each ontology subset. It measures how well the induced ontology's classes align with the
reference one. On Wikidata-TekGen, the induced type is an exact match to the gold class
53.6--58.6\% of the time; where it differs, the remainder splits between a
broader (\textsc{More General}) and a narrower (\textsc{More Narrow})
induced type rather than an unrelated one (\textsc{Not Related} stays
under 10\% on the domain side and under 8\% on the range side for both backbones). These results provide evidence that most
disagreement with the reference ontology is a matter of granularity, not an unrelated branch of the induced hierarchy.

For DBpedia-WebNLG, exact match is lower (27.3--42.4\% across sides and backbones) and \textsc{Not Related} is
higher (11.3--12.2\%, versus under 10\% on Wikidata-TekGen). Even so, granularity mismatches (\textsc{More General}/\textsc{More Narrow})
still account for roughly 80\% of non-exact matches on both sides, so the
induced hierarchy is still placing DBpedia-WebNLG's types in
approximately the right region.

\subsection{MINE}
\label{sec:results-mine}

Table~\ref{tab:mine} reports overall accuracy on MINE (\S\ref{sec:eval-mine}). AutoOntic built with gpt-oss-120b reaches 73.93\% accuracy vs KGGen 66\% with gpt4o (1{,}109/1{,}500 queries). Unlike the Text2KGBench evaluation above, MINE does not score agreement with a reference schema directly. It queries the constructed KG through embedding-similarity retrieval and a fixed-depth graph walk over entities. So this result demonstrates that the typed, canonicalized KG built for a corpus with no supplied schema improves downstream retrieval.

\paragraph{Ontology Quality.} Taken together, Tables~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-separate}--\ref{tab:mine} suggest AutoOntic's schema-free construction recovers a usable typed ontology. Domain/range disagreement with the reference ontology (Table~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-types}) is dominated by granularity mismatches (\textsc{More General}/\textsc{More Narrow}) on both Text2KGBench subsets. Unrelated types (\textsc{Not Related}) stay under 10\% on Wikidata-TekGen and under 13\% on DBpedia-WebNLG, on both the domain and range sides. Together with MINE's retrieval accuracy, this is evidence that the induced hierarchy places types in approximately the right region of the ontology, and that this structure is usable for downstream retrieval, even where it does not converge on the exact reference granularity.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented AutoOntic, a schema-free ontology induction and KG construction pipeline that extends flat knowledge-graph extraction and canonicalization into a typed, hierarchically organized ontology: a hyponymy-aware type hierarchy induced via priority-ordered top-down placement, entity deduplication gated by that hierarchy rather than embedding similarity alone, and relation-level domain/range constraints mined by generalizing observed argument types up the induced hierarchy. The induced ontology's types align closely with the reference schema: over 90\% of AutoOntic’s induced endpoint types are semantically aligned with the reference classes. Matched extractions carry an exact-match type 51–58\% of the time, and where they diverge, this is overwhelmingly a matter of granularity. This indicates that schema-free hierarchy induction recovers structure substantively compatible with a hand-curated ontology. Across intrinsic ontology evaluation (Text2KGBench, \S\ref{sec:results-text2kgbench}) and extrinsic retrieval evaluation (MINE, \S\ref{sec:results-mine}), AutoOntic's schema-free construction recovers a type hierarchy and canonical vocabulary that align substantially with independently-curated reference ontologies and that support effective downstream retrieval.



% Each stage targets a specific failure mode of naively applying flat deduplication to typed structure: conflating synonymy with hyponymy, letting placement order corrupt the resulting hierarchy, and letting relation canonicalization silently merge a predicate with its own grammatical inverse.


\section*{Limitations}

Our evaluation measures agreement between the induced ontology and a benchmark's reference schema, and the usability of the resulting KG for downstream retrieval; it does not directly measure extraction precision, since a schema-free pipeline can correctly extract facts a narrow, curated reference ontology simply does not cover, and such extractions are excluded from recall by design rather than scored as errors. Finally, our evaluation covers English-language corpora and existing benchmark ontologies only; generalization to other languages remains untested.

\section{Sentence Groundedness Analysis}
\label{sec:appendix-groundedness}

Text2KGBench's Wikidata-TekGen sentences are drawn from mid-article Wikipedia text without their source article's title or topic, while its gold triplets are annotated against the full article. The underlying corpus's own construction methodology \citep{agarwal2021tekgen} aligns a Wikidata triple to a sentence by requiring only the \emph{object} entity's alias to occur in it; when the \emph{subject} is absent, a single third-person pronoun is substituted with the subject's canonical name -- but this does not cover zero-anaphora (topic continuity with no pronoun to replace at all), which is the pattern we observe empirically in most cases. To our knowledge this has not been previously quantified specifically for Text2KGBench.
%This class of noise is well documented for distant supervision in relation extraction generally \citep{mintz2009distant, riedel2010modeling}, and DocRED \citep{yao2019docred} reports an analogous statistic built to motivate the opposite fix (document-level rather than sentence-level extraction): at least 40.7\% of its relational facts require reading multiple sentences. 
% though it is consistent with the known limitations of the distant-supervision alignment its underlying corpus uses.

We use an additional GPT-4o judge to classify, per gold triple, whether its sentence alone -- no outside knowledge, no article context -- actually supports it: the subject must be identifiable within the sentence (allowing name-form variation, e.g. ``Charles Spurgeon Johnson'' in the sentence vs.\ ``Charles S. Johnson'' in the reference still counts as grounded), and the object and relationship must actually be stated or implied. Recall in \S\ref{sec:results-text2kgbench} is reported with flagged (ungrounded) triplets excluded from the denominator, as a post-filter over the already-collected judge results requiring no re-extraction or re-matching.

Table~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-grounded} reports recall before and after this filter, per domain (Qwen3-Next-80B): Averaged across Wikidata-TekGen, 48.8\% of gold triplets are flagged ungrounded. Table~\ref{tab:text2kgbench-grounded-split} makes the comparison more direct -- recall computed \emph{separately} over the grounded and the ungrounded subsets, Averaged across all ten domains, for both backbones. Recall on grounded triplets is more than three times that on ungrounded ones for both models, evidence the classification tracks something real rather than adding noise on top of noise: a triple the judge calls grounded is recovered at roughly the rate the pipeline's own extraction and matching quality would predict, while an ungrounded one is only matched when the extractor happens to supply outside knowledge the sentence alone did not license.

\subsection{Entity-Type Cluster Verification}
\label{sec:appendix-cluster-entity-types}
Used during type canonicalization (\S\ref{sec:type-dedup}) to adjudicate a merge/split decision for one candidate cluster of raw entity-type labels, including the hyponymy (``kind of'') test discussed in \S\ref{sec:type-dedup}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for entity-type cluster merge/split verification, including the hyponymy guard.}, label={lst:cluster-entity-types}]
You are an ontology engineer. Below is a group of entity type labels that have been clustered together based on semantic similarity. Some candidates may be annotated with "(context: ...)", describing which relations they tend to appear as the subject/object of in the corpus.

Your task:
1. Decide whether ALL labels refer to the SAME conceptual type.
2. If yes: choose the BEST canonical label (most specific, most common in the field).
3. If no: split the group into sub-groups of labels that DO refer to the same type,  and for each sub-group give a canonical label.

CRITICAL TEST for step 1 -- two labels are the SAME type only if they are simply
two names for the identical concept, interchangeable in any sentence. Before
merging, apply this test: can you naturally say "an X is a kind of Y" (or "a Y,
specifically an X")? If so, they are NOT the same type -- X is a more specific
type and Y is a more general one, and they MUST be split into separate groups,
even though they are obviously related and even though they may share almost
all the same context relations. For example: "documentary film" is a kind of
"film" -> split, never merge, regardless of how similar their contexts look.
"movie" and "film" are just two names for the same thing, with no "kind of"
relationship between them -> merge. Heavy context/relation overlap is expected
and NORMAL for a specific type and its general type (a documentary film
participates in almost every relation "film" does) -- it is NOT by itself
evidence for merging; the "kind of" test always takes priority over context
similarity. When genuinely unsure whether two labels are identical or in a
"kind of" relationship, prefer splitting: a missed merge can still be corrected
later, but a wrong merge silently and permanently destroys the distinction.

Contexts remain useful evidence for the OPPOSITE case: if two labels look
similar but their contexts share no relations in common at all, prefer
splitting them into different (likely unrelated or homonymous) types.

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown), using this format:

If merging all into one type:
{"decision": "merge", "groups": [{"canonical_label": "...", "members": ["...", ...]}]}

If splitting into multiple types:
{"decision": "split", "groups": [{"canonical_label": "...", "members": ["...", ...]}, ...]}"""
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Hierarchy Placement (Parent/Child Checks and Sibling Regrouping)}
\label{sec:appendix-hierarchy-pairwise}
Used by hierarchy induction (\S\ref{sec:hierarchy-induction}) to resolve how one focal type relates to the candidates at its current level. Rather than one combined prompt offering a parent/child/synonym/sibling/none choice, the decision is decomposed into two single-purpose calls -- a parent check, and (only if that finds nothing) a child check -- plus a separate sibling-regrouping call issued only when a level exceeds the candidate batch size $B$ (\S\ref{sec:hierarchy-induction}).

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for the parent check: does the focal type belong under one of the candidates?}, label={lst:hierarchy-parent-check}]
You are an ontology engineer. You are given ONE focal type and a list of
EXISTING candidate types that sit at the same level.

Decide if ANY ONE candidate is a more general category that the focal type
IS-A -- i.e. "every <focal> is a kind of <candidate>" is literally true. This
is the ONLY question. Do not consider whether focal is a parent of any
candidate -- that is asked separately, in a different call.

These are NOT "is-a", no matter how strongly associated the two are:
- focal happens DURING / emerged in / belongs to the era of the candidate
- focal is PART OF, made of, or located in the candidate
- focal is CAUSED BY, produced by, used by, or studied by the candidate
- focal is merely a related topic, or co-occurs with the candidate

If a candidate is not just a broader category but the SAME concept as focal
(a true synonym, e.g. "vehicle" / "means of transport"), say so explicitly --
that is a merge, not a parent relationship.

Some types carry "(context: ...)" -- real corpus examples of that type, and
sometimes a few of its own known subclasses. Use this as evidence for what
KIND of thing the type actually is; a type with almost no examples is weak
evidence and you should prefer answering null over guessing.

Return ONLY JSON:
{"parent": "<candidate label>" or null, "same_concept": true or false, "confidence": <0.0-1.0>}

"same_concept" is only ever true when "parent" is non-null and they are the
identical concept, not merely related by generality.
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for the child check: does the focal type subsume one or more candidates? Only asked if the parent check found nothing.}, label={lst:hierarchy-child-check}]
You are an ontology engineer. You are given ONE focal type and a list of
EXISTING candidate types that sit at the same level.

Decide which candidates, if any, the focal type is a more general category
OVER -- i.e. "every <candidate> is a kind of <focal>" is literally true for
that candidate. Apply this test to EACH candidate SEPARATELY.

These are NOT "is-a", no matter how strongly associated the two are:
- the candidate happens DURING / emerged in / belongs to the era of focal
- the candidate is PART OF, made of, or located in focal
- the candidate is CAUSED BY, produced by, used by, or studied by focal
- the candidate is merely a related topic, or co-occurs with focal

Genuine multi-candidate cases are real but usually small -- two or three
closely related candidates ("war film" and "action film" under "film
genre"). If you find yourself naming most of the list, you are answering a
looser question ("which of these relate to focal?") instead of this one --
stop, and re-apply the test to each candidate on its own.

Some types carry "(context: ...)" -- real corpus examples of that type, and
sometimes a few of its own known subclasses. Use this as evidence for what
KIND of thing the type actually is; a type with almost no examples is weak
evidence, and claiming it as a child on that basis alone is not warranted.

Return ONLY JSON:
{"children": ["<label>", ...], "confidence": <0.0-1.0>}

Empty list if none qualify. confidence applies to the set as a whole.
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for sibling regrouping, issued only when a level's candidate count exceeds $B$.}, label={lst:hierarchy-regroup}]
You are an ontology engineer. You are given a list of types that all sit at
the SAME level -- none is a parent of another -- but there are too many to
compare one at a time, so you must group them first.

For each group of 2 or more types that plausibly share a common, currently
NONEXISTENT parent category (true siblings -- e.g. "war film" and "action
film" both being kinds of film genre), invent ONE short canonical label and a
one-sentence definition for that shared parent.

Rules:
- Only group true SIBLINGS -- same level of abstraction, same kind of thing.
  Do not group merely-related types, and do not group a general type with a
  specific one (that is a parent/child relationship, not a sibling group --
  leave both out of any group).
- A type may appear in AT MOST ONE group.
- Any type that does not clearly belong in a group: leave it out entirely.
  Precision matters more than coverage -- when in doubt, leave it out.
- Before inventing a label, check whether an existing, more general word
  already used elsewhere would describe the group just as well. If so,
  prefer reusing a short, ordinary word over a novel compound.

Return ONLY JSON:
{"groups": [{"label": "...", "definition": "...", "members": ["<exact input label>", ...]}, ...]}

Empty list if nothing groups. Every member list must have length >= 2.
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Entity-Name Cluster Verification}
\label{sec:appendix-cluster-entity-names}
Used during entity deduplication (\S\ref{sec:entity-dedup}) to adjudicate a merge/split decision for one candidate cluster of compound \texttt{name~[type]} entity mentions, within one parent-gated partition.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for entity-name cluster merge/split verification.}, label={lst:cluster-entity-names}]
You are a knowledge graph engineer. Below is a group of entity mentions, each in the format "name [type]". These were clustered together by embedding similarity ONLY -- being in this list is NOT evidence that they are the same entity. Most clusters contain several DIFFERENT entities that merely sound alike or share a type.

Your task:
1. Decide which mentions are SYNONYMOUS: different ways of writing the SAME single real-world entity.
2. Group synonymous entities together. A mention with no synonym forms its own group of one.
3. For each group, choose the BEST canonical name -- the most complete, unambiguous form of that entity's name, taken from the mentions themselves. The canonical name must be JUST the entity name, WITHOUT the [type] bracket.
4. Each group's "members" array must contain the EXACT compound strings as provided (including the [type] part).

### The test for a duplicate

Two mentions are SYNONYMOUS ONLY if they are denoting the same person, organization, place, work, event, or concept -- such that **every statement true of one is true of the other**, and merging them would LOSE NO INFORMATION.

Apply it by substitution: if you swapped one mention for the other in a sentence, would the meaning stay identical?
- "Chris Nolan" / "Christopher Nolan" -- identical meaning. SYNONYMOUS.
- "Nobel Prize" / "Nobel Prize in Physics" -- one is the award family, the other a specific award. Merging discards "in Physics". NOT SYNONYMOUS.

### Type rules

- SIMILAR types allow merging only if the entity name, combined with the type, refers to the same real-world entity ("Christopher Nolan [film director]" / "Chris Nolan [filmmaker]" / "Nolan [human]").
- Abbreviations, nicknames, spelling and case variants, and partial names merge with their full form when the type context supports it.

### Do not invent a name

The canonical name must be one of the given mentions (or its fuller spelling). Never invent a broader umbrella term to cover a group -- if you find yourself reaching for a general word that covers several mentions, that is proof they are NOT duplicates and must stay separate.

When in doubt, keep entities SEPARATE. Precision matters far more than recall here: a wrong merge destroys two entities permanently, while a missed merge leaves both intact.

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown). Every input mention must appear in exactly one group:
{"groups": [{"canonical_label": "<just the name>", "members": ["<name [type]>", ...]}, ...]}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Relation-Direction Classification}
\label{sec:appendix-relation-direction}
Used by constraint induction's Stage~1 (\S\ref{sec:constraints}) to classify each raw surface form merged into a canonical relation as \emph{forward} or \emph{inverted} relative to that relation's own canonical direction, ahead of domain/range constraint mining.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={System prompt for relation-direction (forward/inverted) classification.}, label={lst:relation-direction}]
You are an ontology engineer resolving relation directionality.

Below is a canonical relation (its chosen representative label) and the full list of raw
predicate surface forms that were merged into it during relation canonicalization. Surface forms
are merged when they denote the same underlying relationship between two entities, INCLUDING
cases where one form is the grammatical passive/inverse of another (e.g. "directed" and "was
directed by" describe the same relationship, just told with subject and object swapped).

Your task: classify EACH surface form relative to the canonical label's own direction, using
exactly one of:

1. "forward" -- this surface form keeps the same subject/object roles as the canonical label.
   Example: canonical "directed", surface form "helmed" -> "forward" (same roles: person directs
   film).

2. "inverted" -- this surface form swaps subject and object relative to the canonical label: what
   is the SUBJECT under the canonical label becomes the OBJECT under this surface form, and vice
   versa. Example: canonical "directed" (subject=director, object=film), surface form "was
   directed by" -> "inverted" (under this form, the film is the grammatical subject and the
   director the object).

The canonical label itself is always "forward" by definition -- it is the reference direction
everything else is judged against.

Base this purely on grammatical/semantic role structure, not on which surface form is more
frequent or "sounds more natural" -- frequency has nothing to do with direction.

Return ONLY valid JSON (no explanation, no markdown), using this format:
{
  "labels": {
    "directed": "forward",
    "was directed by": "inverted",
    "helmed": "forward"
  }
}

Every surface form given to you must appear as a key in "labels", with exactly one of "forward"
or "inverted" as its value. Do not invent surface forms that were not given to you.
\end{lstlisting}
